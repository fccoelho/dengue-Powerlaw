\title{From exponent to risk: power-law tails and the probability of\\
large dengue epidemics in Brazil}
\author{Fl\'avio Code\c{c}o Coelho}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Power-law tails are widely reported for dengue and read as a verdict on
forecastability: with an exponent near two, extremes are held to be unpredictable. We
show that it specifies risk instead, and test it out of sample on weekly data for
5{,}571 Brazilian municipalities (2010--2026), fitting discrete power laws with
Kolmogorov--Smirnov selection of the lower bound under five constructions of the same
series (50{,}391 fits) against an exponential.

Construction determines the verdict: median exponents range from $1.95$ to $2.08$, and
the same counts support a power law in 77\% of municipalities pooled over a decade
against 23\% within single seasons. Epidemic size, the annual total of the episode,
retains a genuine tail: the power law is preferred in all 16
national cross-sections (median $\alpha=2.03$) and in 96\% of 353 state-years (median
$\alpha=1.98$), with lower bounds two to three orders of magnitude above the bulk.

Because $\alpha\approx2$ fixes the exceedance curve, the pair is a two-parameter risk
specification: 5.6--16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times the lower bound in a
given year, 0.3--2.7\% a hundred times it. The exponent is rank-correlated with that
bound at $0.89$ nationally and $0.64$ within states, an association absent in
simulation.

Framed as exceedance, prediction is feasible where the panel resolves it: at municipal
level, with the label referred to each unit's own history, lagged descriptors,
including duration and peak timing, improve the Brier score over calibrated
persistence in 4 of 5 origins and are significantly the worse in the fifth, the only
contrast satisfying the pre-specified rule, and raise the area under the ROC curve from
$0.50$ to $0.58$, the full ladder reaching $0.65$; at state level the same contrast
holds in 2 of 5 origins, where the label is pooled across units. The scaling block
satisfies the rule at neither level; for size its median differential at $\tau=0.95$,
$+4.9\%$, excludes zero in 2 origins in opposite directions. The tail belongs in a
risk curve, not in a predictor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The dynamics of infectious disease epidemics have generated an extensive
literature, both in mechanistic mathematical modelling and in statistical
inference \citep{siettos2013mathematical}. The former seeks to represent the
causal chain of transmission; the latter seeks to characterise the distribution
of observed incidence.

Among the most influential results of the mechanistic tradition is the basic
reproduction number $R_0$, which governs the speed of spread and defines the
epidemic threshold; in a vector-borne infection the threshold quantity rests on a
generation interval that varies with temperature, and must be estimated
accordingly \citep{codeco2018rt}. Because such models are seldom identifiable
from incidence alone, their parameters are usually obtained within a calibration
framework that returns a distribution rather than a point estimate
\citep{coelho2011bayesian}. In the statistical tradition, incidence is most often
modelled as a stochastic process, typically Poisson or negative binomial
\citep{becker1999statistical, li2012log}. The central difficulty in either case
is overdispersion: surveillance counts generate far more extreme observations
than tractable count models admit.

A recurrent claim is that this overdispersion is not merely large but
\emph{scale-free}, that incidence follows a power law
\citep{rhodes1997critical, clauset2009power, neipel2020power}. Where transmission
is itself power-law-tailed the exponent is not a descriptive curiosity: the
survival probability of an outbreak obeys a scaling law whose form is set by that
exponent \citep{falco2022finite}. The claim matters because a heavy tail implies
extremes that most models fail to predict, and because those extremes account for
most of the disease burden and most of its economic cost. The quantity that public
health planning acts on is the attack ratio, the fraction of the population
infected in a season, which has to be inferred from aggregated notification data
because serotype-specific immunity is unobserved \citep{coelho2015attack}. Were
the claim correct, it would bear directly on forecasting and on the allocation of
resources.

The empirical record is inconsistent. Reported exponents for dengue vary widely,
some studies find exponential rather than power-law behaviour, and the presence
of a heavy tail appears to depend on the study period, on the spatial unit and on
the outcome variable. This heterogeneity has generally been attributed to
biology or to geography, on the assumption that different places or different
years are governed by different regimes. In Brazil the geography has itself moved:
dengue has expanded towards higher latitudes and towards municipalities with no
prior transmission, so that the set of units entering a cross-section changes from
one season to the next \citep{codeco2022expansion}.

We test a narrower set of claims, and we begin from a different premise: that a
power law is not a verdict on forecastability but a \emph{specification of risk}.
An exponent of two assigns a determinate probability to every exceedance level,
so that the fitted pair $(\alpha, x_{\min})$ summarises the entire risk curve of
a municipality using two parameters --- in a setting where the observables vary by
two orders of magnitude between years. What a heavy tail precludes is that the
\emph{magnitude} of the largest event be estimated from the variance; what it
supplies is the probability that such an event occurs. These are distinct
problems, and a proper scoring rule keeps them distinct.

\subsection{Questions addressed}
\label{sec:questions}

We pose three questions in sequence, each conditional on the answer to the
preceding one.

First, \emph{what is the exponent an exponent of?} Any epidemiological series
admits several legitimate partitionings --- the weekly counts of one
municipality, its annual epidemic totals, the set of epidemic sizes across
municipalities within one season --- and these are different random variables
with different supports. We fit the same estimator to the same surveillance data
under five constructions and report the exponent together with the diagnostic
($x_{\min}$) that makes it interpretable. Epidemic size, the quantity
surveillance services act on, is the construction that survives. Epidemic
duration and peak timing are not submitted to that test: both are bounded by the
length of the season, so a scale-free law cannot hold for them a priori, and the
test would return a verdict about the estimator rather than about the epidemic.

Second, \emph{what does the fitted pair provide?} If $(\alpha, x_{\min})$ is a
risk curve, it should reproduce the empirical frequency of large seasons. We test
that directly against the same cross-sections.

Third, \emph{does the tail improve forecasts?} We pose the question in the two
forms in which it could plausibly succeed: as a classification of whether a
municipality will exceed its own historical upper quartile, which is the risk
statement itself; and as a quantile regression of epidemic size. Epidemic
duration and peak timing enter not as objects of a scaling claim but as ordinary
predictors, available before the season, which may or may not be informative.
The evaluation uses a rolling-origin design, a four-rung ablation ladder and a
decision rule fixed in advance.

\subsection{Contributions}


\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A robust exponent for epidemic size, reported with the diagnostic
that makes it interpretable.} Under five constructions of the same surveillance
series, the two weekly constructions show what the diagnostic is for: $x_{\min}$
collapses to five cases or fewer in 77\% of \lvl{W-mun} and 81\% of \lvl{W-seas} fits,
so that the power law is estimated on counts of one and two. The size
constructions do not collapse, and they agree: the power law is preferred in 16
of 16 national cross-sections and in 96\% of 353 state-year cross-sections, with
median exponents of $2.03$ and $1.98$ and median lower bounds of 730 and 169
cases respectively. Reporting $x_{\min}$ beside every exponent is what separates
the fits that describe a tail from those that describe the bulk of the data.

\item \textbf{The fitted pair as a calibrated risk curve.} Equation
\eqref{eq:exceed} maps $(\alpha, x_{\min})$ to $\Pr(S \ge s)$ for every $s$, and
the national fits reproduce the observed exceedance frequency over two decades of
epidemic size. This is a decision-relevant output that a point forecast does not
provide, and it is obtained from two parameters.

\item \textbf{A measured coupling that identifies what the exponent
represents.} Within units, $\alpha$ and $\log_{10} x_{\min}$ are rank-correlated
at $0.64$ (median over 24 states) and at $0.89$ across national cross-sections,
whereas a simulation with the exponent held fixed recovers no such association.
The pair is therefore a low-variance summary of seasonal magnitude: informative
about size, but not independent of lagged incidence, which a reading of the
exponent as an independent predictor would require.

\item \textbf{An out-of-sample answer in the form in which the question can be
answered.} The probability of a large season is predictable where the panel is
large enough to resolve it: lagged descriptors improve the Brier score over
calibrated persistence in 4 of the 5 municipal forecast origins, the only
contrast in this study to satisfy the pre-specified rule, whereas at state level
the same rung fails on 23 to 25 units per origin. The scaling block adds no
incremental skill to either task --- 0 of 5 origins for size, 1 of 5
for municipal exceedance --- and leaves the upper quantile of the size forecast
where it was. The scaling description is thus justified as a risk statement and
not as a predictor.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data sources and study period}
\label{sec:data}

We analyse weekly dengue case counts assembled by the InfoDengue/Mosqlimate
platform \citep{araujo2025large} for the period from 1 January 2010 to 22 January
2026, for every Brazilian municipality represented in the platform. The analysed
variable is the nowcast-adjusted estimated number of cases, that is, the
platform's reconstruction of the number of infections occurring in each week
after correcting for reporting delay by a hierarchical model of the delay
distribution \citep{bastos2019reporting}; it is not the raw notification count. The
series were projected onto a common epidemiological week ending on Sunday. Weeks
without a reported case are retained in the series but excluded from
distributional fits, since the power-law family is defined on positive support.

Descriptors of each epidemic season were obtained from the platform's seasonal
decomposition, which partitions a municipal series into epidemic episodes and
reports, for each episode, its total number of cases, its duration in weeks and
the week in which it peaked. These descriptors are available for 16 years, 2011
to 2026, the last of them bounded by the data cut-off of 22 January 2026; the
fit pooled over all years is reported separately and is excluded from every year
count, so that each national cross-section corresponds to one calendar season.
Only epidemic size is fitted as a power law; duration and peak timing enter the
analysis as predictors of size.

Municipal population figures are the estimates distributed with the platform
(5{,}571 municipalities, one record with a zero entry excluded). Population enters
no power-law fit; it is used to compute the descriptive correlations of
Section~\ref{sec:coupling} and, as $\log_{10}$ population, as one predictor in
the forecasting models of Section~\ref{sec:targets}.

\subsection{Definitions and notation}
\label{sec:definitions}

Several quantities recur throughout the paper, and their meaning is fixed here
once and for all. Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the notation.

\emph{Weekly count.} Let $c_{i,w}$ denote the estimated number of dengue cases
in municipality $i$ during epidemiological week $w$. It is the primitive
observation on which every construction in this paper is built.

\emph{Season.} A season, or epidemic year, $t$ is the set of epidemiological
weeks assigned to calendar year $t$; it contains at most 53 weeks. A season is
indexed by the calendar year of its weeks, not by the year in which an epidemic
peaks.

\emph{Epidemic size.} Let $S_{i,t}$ denote the total number of cases in the
epidemic episode of municipality $i$ in season $t$, as identified by the seasonal
decomposition. We refer to $S_{i,t}$ as the \emph{epidemic size}, and we use
\emph{annual total} as a synonym for it and for no other quantity. In particular,
$S_{i,t}$ is neither the weekly count $c_{i,w}$ nor the calendar-year sum of
$c_{i,w}$, which would also include cases notified outside any identified
episode. The distinction is operational as well as definitional: an episode is
identified only where the decomposition detects one, so municipality-seasons
without a detected episode contribute no observation to the size distribution,
whereas the weekly counts exist for every municipality and week.

\emph{Cross-section.} A cross-section at season $t$ is the set $\{S_{i,t}\}$ of
epidemic sizes of the municipalities within a stated spatial scope. The
\emph{national cross-section} contains every Brazilian municipality with an
identified episode in $t$; a \emph{state-year cross-section} contains the
municipalities of one state in one season. A cross-section is therefore a sample
of units at a fixed time, in contrast to a series, which is a sample of times at
a fixed unit.

\emph{Construction.} A construction is the triple (fitted variable, sampling
unit, dimension of pooling) that determines which random variable is estimated
and what each observation of the fitted sample represents.

\emph{Lower bound and bulk.} The lower bound $x_{\min}$ is the threshold above
which the power-law form is assumed to hold, and the \emph{bulk} is the part of
the empirical distribution below it. A fit is \emph{tail-informative} when
$x_{\min}$ lies above the bulk, in the operational sense of
Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic}.

\emph{Duration and peak timing.} $D_{i,t}$ denotes the duration of the episode
in weeks and $P_{i,t}$ the epidemiological week of its maximum.

\subsection{Five aggregation levels}
\label{sec:levels}

The unit of analysis is the sample of positive observations passed to the
estimator. Table~\ref{tab:levels} defines the five aggregation levels used; each is
a construction in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:definitions}, and the two terms are
used interchangeably.
Two variables are fitted, both derived from the same municipal series: the weekly
count $c_{i,w}$ at constructions \lvl{W-mun} and \lvl{W-seas}, and the epidemic size $S_{i,t}$ at
constructions \lvl{S-mun}, \lvl{S-state} and \lvl{S-nat}. Within each group the constructions differ only in
the dimension along which the variable is pooled, which is what allows a
contrast between two of them to be read as the effect of that dimension alone.

Every handle is read as a variable and a scope. The initial letter names the
fitted variable --- \emph{W} for the weekly count, \emph{S} for the epidemic size
--- and the remainder names the scope over which observations are pooled:
\emph{mun} for a single municipality, \emph{seas} for a single season of a single
municipality, \emph{state} for the municipalities of one state in one season, and
\emph{nat} for the municipalities of the country in one season. The handles name
constructions and are not random variables: only $c_{i,w}$ and $S_{i,t}$ enter the
likelihood. They are used throughout for the five constructions and also label the
panels of Figures~\ref{fig:alpha}, \ref{fig:verdicts} and \ref{fig:xmin}.

A construction is fixed by two independent decisions. The first selects the
variable that enters the likelihood: the weekly count $c_{i,w}$ as observed, or
the season total $S_{i,t}$, obtained by summing the weeks of the epidemic episode
delimited by the seasonal decomposition described in Section~\ref{sec:data}. The
second selects the sample over which that variable is pooled: the weeks of one
municipality, taken over the complete series or over a single season; the seasons
of one municipality; or the municipalities of one state or of the whole country
within a single season. The five constructions of Table~\ref{tab:levels} are the
combinations in use, not the full crossing of the two decisions; a pairing
omitted from the table, such as the weekly counts of a national cross-section, is
one that no contrast in this paper requires.

Constructions \lvl{W-mun} and \lvl{W-seas} differ only in the temporal window over which the weekly
counts are pooled: \lvl{W-mun} uses the complete available series of a municipality
(January 2010 to January 2026), whereas \lvl{W-seas} restricts the sample to a single
season, of at most 53 weeks. Their sample sizes differ by roughly an order of
magnitude, which is why the comparison between them is made on shares rather than
on counts.

Constructions \lvl{S-mun}, \lvl{S-state} and \lvl{S-nat} fit the same variable, $S_{i,t}$, and differ only in
the dimension of pooling. \lvl{S-mun} pools the seasons of one municipality and is
therefore a statement about how a single municipality's epidemic sizes vary over
time; a municipality enters this construction only if at least ten seasons are
available. \lvl{S-state} and \lvl{S-nat} pool municipalities within a period, at state and national
scope respectively, and are therefore statements about how sizes vary across
space in a given season. This nesting is deliberate: each contrast reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results} is a single step within one of the two groups, so that a
difference in the inferred regime is attributable to the construction rather than
to a change of data source, period or variable.

\subsection{Discrete power-law estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

For each sample we fit a discrete power law by maximum likelihood following
\citet{clauset2009power}. The fitted probability mass function for integer
$x \ge x_{\min}$ is
%
\begin{equation}
p(x) = \frac{x^{-\alpha}}{\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})},
\label{eq:pl}
\end{equation}
%
where $\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})$ is the generalised (Hurwitz) zeta function, which
normalises the discrete mass. The exponent $\alpha$ is estimated by numerical
maximisation of the likelihood, and the lower bound $x_{\min}$ is selected
jointly by minimising the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the empirical and
fitted cumulative distributions over all candidate values of $x_{\min}$. Because
the observations are counts, the discrete formulation is used throughout; the
continuous approximation is not employed at any construction.

One property of the estimator must be stated for the table of results to be read
correctly. The numerical optimisation constrains $\alpha$ to the interval
$(1, 3]$: an estimate cannot exceed $3$, and a sample whose likelihood is
maximised beyond that value returns $\alpha=3$ rather than its unconstrained
maximum. The bound is immaterial for epidemic size, the variable on which the
substantive conclusions rest. At the size constructions no fit approaches it, the
largest values being $2.94$ at state level and $2.25$ at national level; at the
two weekly constructions it is reached by 12 of the 48{,}937 fits, or
$0.02\%$. The exponents reported in this paper are accordingly unconstrained
maxima and not boundary values.

The choice of $x_{\min}$ is not a nuisance parameter. It determines which
fraction of the data the model actually describes, and therefore whether the
fitted exponent refers to a tail at all. We therefore retain $x_{\min}$ for every
fit and report its median and the proportion of fits with $x_{\min} \le 5$ at
each construction.

\subsection{Comparison against an alternative distribution}
\label{sec:comparison}

A power law can always be fitted; the question is whether it describes the data
better than a plausible alternative. We compare each power-law fit against an
exponential distribution, which is the natural null for epidemiological counts
because it corresponds to a constant hazard of termination and generates a light
tail. The comparison follows the normalised likelihood-ratio procedure of
\citet{clauset2009power},
%
\begin{equation}
R = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\ln p_{\mathrm{PL}}(x_i) - \ln p_{\mathrm{exp}}(x_i)\right],
\label{eq:lr}
\end{equation}
%
normalised so that its magnitude is comparable across samples of different size.
The sign of $R$ indicates the preferred model, with $R>0$ favouring the power law
and $R<0$ the exponential, and its associated $p$-value quantifies whether the
sign is distinguishable from sampling noise.

We adopt a deliberately conservative reporting convention. A construction is
described as \emph{power-law supported} only when $R>0$ and $p<0.05$, as
\emph{exponential supported} only when $R<0$ and $p<0.05$, and as
\emph{inconclusive} otherwise. This prevents the common error of counting a
positive but non-significant $R$ as evidence for scaling.

\subsection{Identification diagnostic}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

A fit is flagged as \emph{tail-informative} when $x_{\min}$ exceeds the bulk of
the observed distribution, operationalised as $x_{\min}>5$ cases for weekly
counts and as $x_{\min}$ above the median observed value for the size
constructions. Where $x_{\min} \le 5$, the fitted power law is being asked to
describe the entire empirical distribution, including the small-count regime in
which the discrete power law is not a plausible generative model, since counts of
one or two cases arise from reporting mechanics rather than from scale-free
branching. Such fits are retained in the analysis but excluded from substantive
interpretation, and the proportion concerned is reported explicitly.

\subsection{From description to prediction: the exceedance curve}
\label{sec:bridge}

A fitted power law is not merely a description of a histogram; it is a statement
about exceedance probabilities. For integer $s \ge x_{\min}$,
%
\begin{equation}
\Pr(S \ge s) \;=\; \frac{\zeta(\alpha,s)}{\zeta(\alpha,x_{\min})},
\label{eq:exceed}
\end{equation}
%
so that a single pair $(\alpha, x_{\min})$ parameterises the probability that a
municipality experiences an epidemic of at least any given size. Four
consequences of Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed} shape the design of the forecasting
evaluation.

First, the exponent summarises \emph{risk} rather than \emph{activity}. Two
municipalities with the same expected annual incidence but different $\alpha$
face different probabilities of a catastrophic season, and that difference is
invisible to the mean. The fitted models quantify the claim rather than merely
asserting it: applied to the 16 national size fits, Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed}
implies that between 5.6\% and 16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times
$x_{\min}$ in a given year, and that between 0.3\% and 2.7\% exceed one hundred
times $x_{\min}$. These are falsifiable implications of the description, and
Figure~\ref{fig:exceed} confronts them with the empirical exceedance frequencies.

Second, $\alpha$ is a low-variance quantity \emph{relative to its target}. Across
the national cross-sections it takes values between $1.78$ and $2.25$, and it is
essentially unrelated to municipality size ($r=-0.043$). Annual case counts over
the same period vary by two orders of magnitude. The dispersion of
$\hat\alpha$ is not zero --- it exceeds sampling noise by roughly a factor of five
in standard deviation, and it co-varies with the selected $x_{\min}$
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}) --- but it is small along precisely the dimension in
which the target is volatile. That is the specific reason to carry $\alpha$ into
the predictive models rather than treating it as a descriptive curiosity, and the
same coupling is the reason the ablation, rather than the correlation, has to
decide the question.

Third, $\alpha\approx2$ has a direct methodological consequence for evaluation.
The second moment of a power law with $\alpha \le 2$ does not exist in the
continuous limit, and at $\alpha \approx 2$ the sample mean is unstable, so that
squared-error losses are decided by the few largest outbreaks. A model that is
adequate everywhere except in the tail can therefore appear excellent or
inadequate depending on which years fall in the test set. We consequently
evaluate on quantile (pinball) losses and on the classification of tail events,
and treat the root mean squared error as a secondary, tail-dominated diagnostic.

Finally, the predictive hypothesis is stated in a form that can fail. If
$(\alpha, x_{\min})$ carries information about the coming season beyond what
lagged counts already encode, a model containing them should outperform an
otherwise identical model without them, out of sample. If instead the exponent is
close to a deterministic function of the lower moments of the same series, the
two models should be indistinguishable, and the descriptive result would carry no
forecasting content. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} specifies the comparison that
decides between these outcomes, and Section~\ref{sec:leakage} lists the four ways
in which such a comparison could be falsified and how each is prevented.

\subsection{Predictive target and predictors}
\label{sec:targets}

The forecasting problem is posed at the level of an administrative unit, a
municipality or a state, and a target season $t$: every predictor is measured in
season $t-1$ or earlier, and the target is a property of the season of year $t$.
There is one target, the epidemic size $S_t$ defined in
Section~\ref{sec:definitions}, because it is the quantity surveillance services
act on and the only one of the three seasonal descriptors with unbounded support.
Epidemic duration $D_t$ and peak timing $P_t$ are predictors, not outcomes: they
describe the phenology of the previous season, and the ladder asks whether that
phenology helps forecast the magnitude of the next one.

Predictors fall into six blocks, listed in Table~\ref{tab:features}: lagged
seasonal descriptors of the previous season, which include its duration and peak
timing; the scaling descriptors $(\alpha_{S,t-1}, x_{\min,S,t-1})$ obtained from
the power-law fit to the previous season; the historical trend
$\dot\alpha_{t-1}$, defined as the ordinary least-squares slope of $\alpha_S$ over
all seasons strictly before $t$ and set to zero where fewer than two such seasons
exist; the epidemiological driver $R_{0,t-1}$ reported by the seasonal
decomposition; $\log_{10}$ population; and eight quarterly climate aggregates
from year $t-1$, namely mean minimum temperature and mean minimum humidity in each
of the four quarters. Units lacking the mandatory lags, that is without a previous
season, are excluded rather than imputed; the optional $\dot\alpha$ is set to zero
where it is undefined.

The ladder of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} groups these six blocks into three
cumulative rungs. M1 carries the lagged descriptors, the epidemiological driver and
population; M2 adds the climate block; M3 adds the two scaling blocks. Duration and
peak timing therefore enter at the first rung, as part of the lagged descriptors,
and each contrast between consecutive rungs is attributable to the block that the
later rung adds.

\subsection{Regression estimator}
\label{sec:regression}

The choice of estimator is a design decision in this problem and not a tuning
detail, because the quantity of interest can lie outside the range of the data to
which the model is fitted. Under $\alpha \approx 2$ the season of greatest
public-health interest is systematically larger than any season in the training
record, so the forecast must be allowed to take a value beyond the largest
response observed. The estimator is therefore a quantile regression on a
cubic spline basis with linear boundary extrapolation
\citep{koenker1978regression}. It minimises the pinball loss. Outside the
observed range the boundary segment continues linearly, which on the
$\log(1+S)$ scale is a multiplicative extrapolation on the scale of cases. The two
predictors expressed in cases, $S_{t-1}$ and $x_{\min,t-1}$, are logged before
entering the basis, without which the extrapolation would be linear in the wrong
scale. The $L_1$ penalty is $10^{-4}$, deliberately weak.

Quantile models are fitted on the grid
$\tau \in \{0.05, 0.10, \dots, 0.95\}$, which yields a full predictive
distribution per unit and season rather than a point forecast, and all stochastic
components were seeded identically across runs. Figure~\ref{fig:predobs} shows
the resulting out-of-sample forecasts against the observed totals. The boundary
segment is what allows a forecast to be placed above the largest annual total in
the training window, the region where the seasons of greatest concern lie.

\subsection{Classification of large seasons}
\label{sec:classifier}

The second predictive task is posed as a decision problem rather than as an
estimation problem: will unit $i$ experience, in season $t$, a season larger than
the $p$-th percentile of its own historical distribution? The target is
%
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,t} \;=\; \mathbb{I}\!\left[\,S_{i,t} > Q_p\big(\{S_{i,k}\}_{k<t}\big)\,\right],
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
%
with $p=0.75$ throughout. The threshold is a within-fold quantity: it is
recomputed from the training seasons of each fold and of each split, never from
the full sample, so that the label of a test observation cannot depend on
information unavailable to the model. The same discipline governs the
class-imbalance weight, set to the ratio of negative to positive observations in
the training fold.

The scope over which the percentile is taken is set by what each level can support.
At municipal level $Q_p$ is computed from each unit's own training seasons, and
3{,}976 of the 5{,}571 municipalities supply the five seasons that estimate requires.
At state level the panel supplies 23 to 25 units per origin, too few for a within-unit
percentile, and $Q_p$ is taken across the states of the fold's training seasons. The
two labels therefore answer slightly different questions, and the state-level task is
the closer of the two to a ranking by unit size (Section~\ref{sec:classresults}).

The classifier is a penalised logistic regression on the same spline basis, with
the class imbalance handled by weighting the training rows by the
negative-to-positive ratio. As with the regressor, the specification must remain
informative beyond the range covered by the training rows, and the logistic form
does so by extrapolating linearly in log-odds, so that a season far outside the
range of the panel is not forced towards the base rate observed in training.
Validation is an expanding-window split defined on the season index: fold $j$
trains on every unit-season earlier than $v_j$ and validates on season $v_j$
alone, where the $v_j$ are the $k=\min\{5,\, n_{\text{seasons}}-2\}$ most recent
training seasons. A fold
therefore exists only once at least two earlier seasons are available, and the
number of folds is capped at five. Because the folds are a function of the season
index rather than of arbitrary ordering, they are invariant to the ordering of the
observations.

This distinction is not cosmetic, and it is the step of the design whose failure
is silent. A split defined by arbitrary ordering rather than by the season index
produces, in a frame ordered by unit, a grouping by municipality: each fold then
trains on some municipalities across the whole calendar and validates on others
over the same calendar, so that validation contains no season absent from
training. The quantity estimated becomes transportability to new municipalities
rather than temporal generalisation, and the estimate depends on an undeclared
ordering of the rows. The defect is narrower but not absent when the frame
happens to be ordered by season, since contiguous blocks then still cut through
calendar years. The split is therefore indexed explicitly by the season, and any
validation season that is not strictly later than the training seasons of its
fold is treated as an error rather than as a warning. The decision threshold
applied to the predicted probability is chosen by Youden's $J$ statistic
\citep{youden1950index}, computed on the out-of-fold predictions rather than on
the training fit, and then held fixed for the test set. We report the area under
the ROC curve, the area under the precision--recall curve, which is the more
informative summary under class imbalance, the Brier score
\citep{gneiting2007strictly}, the $F_1$ score at the Youden threshold and a
calibration curve, and we test differences in the area under the curve between
nested predictor sets by the method of \citet{delong1988comparing}.

Feature attribution is measured by held-out permutation importance on the Brier
score, reported both per predictor and for the scaling block permuted as a unit
\citep{breiman2001random}: under the collinearity documented in
Section~\ref{sec:coupling}, a per-column ranking alone understates the importance
of a block whose members can substitute for one another.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and ablation ladder}
\label{sec:ablation}

A single temporal split cannot distinguish a predictor that helps from a
predictor that happened to help once. We therefore adopt a rolling-origin design:
for each forecast origin $y \in \{2021, \dots, 2025\}$ the model is trained on all
observations with season earlier than $y$ and evaluated on season $y$, and the
per-origin losses are pooled. This provides five out-of-sample seasons and allows
skill to be read as a function of the history available to the model.

Within each origin, the incremental value of the scaling description is isolated
by the ablation ladder of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. Each rung adds one block of
predictors to the preceding one, so that a contrast between consecutive rungs is
attributable to the block that was added. The rung of substantive interest is
M3 against M2, which differ \emph{only} in the presence of
$(\alpha_{S,t-1}, x_{\min,S,t-1}, \dot\alpha_{t-1})$.

Forecast values at M0 are scaled by the empirical $\tau$-quantile of the
within-unit season-on-season ratio, estimated on the training seasons, so that the
same benchmark can be evaluated at every quantile level rather than only at the
median. At $\tau=0.5$ the scale factor is close to unity and M0 reduces to plain
persistence; at the other levels this is the honest analogue of persistence and
not a deliberately weak comparator.

M0 is deliberately naive, because a benchmark that is already sophisticated
manufactures skill for whatever is added to it. Skill is reported as
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SS} \;=\; 1-\frac{L(\text{model})}{L(\mathrm{M0})},
\label{eq:skill}
\end{equation}
%
on the primary loss of each task: the pinball loss at $\tau=0.5$ for the
regression target and the Brier score for the classifier, both of which are proper
scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007strictly}. Secondary losses are the mean absolute
error and the root mean squared error of the point forecasts, the mean absolute
scaled error of \citet{hyndman2006another} to make errors comparable across units
of very different size, and the pinball loss at each $\tau$ of the grid.

Because units differ by orders of magnitude in scale, all model-versus-model
comparisons are made on paired loss differentials computed within the same unit
and season; their uncertainty is estimated by a bootstrap that resamples
\emph{units} rather than observations, preserving the within-unit correlation
across seasons, with 2{,}000 replicates. For the comparison pooled across origins
we additionally report the Diebold--Mariano statistic \citep{diebold1995comparing}
with a Newey--West correction, since the loss series are serially correlated by
construction.

The decision rule is fixed in advance. The scaling block is judged to add
predictive value only if M3 improves on M2 on the primary loss with a 95\%
bootstrap interval for the paired difference that excludes zero, in the same
direction, for at least 3 of the 5 forecast origins. This is stricter than
a single significant pooled test, and deliberately so: a pooled test can be
carried by one anomalous season, which is precisely the failure mode that a
heavy-tailed target invites.

\subsection{Leakage controls}
\label{sec:leakage}

Four sources of leakage are structurally possible in this design, and each is
closed explicitly.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Contemporaneous scaling descriptors.} $\alpha_{S,t}$ is estimated
from the weekly counts of season $t$, which are part of the target. Only
$\alpha_{S,t-1}$ enters as a predictor, and $\dot\alpha_{t-1}$ is computed
exclusively from seasons strictly before $t$.
\item \textbf{The classification threshold.} $Q_p$ is computed inside each fold
from training seasons only. Computing it once on the full sample would leak the
distribution of the test seasons into the labels of the training seasons,
inflating apparent skill by construction.
\item \textbf{Cross-sectional information.} The cross-section of municipalities
within season $t$ is an outcome of that season and is therefore never a
predictor; the scaling descriptors always come from the previous season's
cross-section.
\item \textbf{The validation split itself.} Folds are defined on the season index
(Section~\ref{sec:classifier}), so that no fold trains on a season at or after the
season it validates on. This is the only one of the four channels that fails
silently in a plausible implementation --- every number still resembles a
respectable validation score --- and it is therefore enforced rather than merely
documented.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Epidemic size follows a power law with an exponent near two}
\label{sec:size}

The construction that survives both criteria is the one that matters
operationally: the distribution of epidemic size across units within a season. At
the national cross-section, all 16 annual fits favour the power law and all are
significant, with median $\alpha=2.03$ (IQR 1.90--2.10) and
median $x_{\min}=730$ cases. The exponent is stable, ranging from 1.78 to 2.25 over
sixteen seasons in a period in which annual case counts vary by two orders of
magnitude. At state level the picture is consistent and weaker in
significance, as expected from smaller cross-sections: the power law is preferred
in 96\% of the 353 state-year cross-sections and significant in 52\%, with median
$\alpha=1.98$ and median $x_{\min}=169$ cases.

This is the object with which the remainder of the paper works, and the reason is
worth stating precisely. The median lower bound of 730 cases at national level
and 169 at state level places the fit well above the bulk of a distribution whose
observations span four orders of magnitude: what is described is the upper region
in which the risk is located, not the body of small seasons. This is also the
sense in which the two groups of constructions are not interchangeable. At
$x_{\min}=2$ the fitted law describes counts of one and two cases; at
$x_{\min}=730$ it describes the upper decade of a distribution. Two analyses
reported as agreeing on the exponent can therefore be describing entirely
different objects: \lvl{W-mun} and \lvl{S-mun} have median exponents of $1.95$ and $2.08$, a
difference of $0.13$, while their median lower bounds differ by a factor of
$150$. The empirical complementary cumulative distribution of
Figure~\ref{fig:ccdf} shows the same fact graphically, as a straight line over two
decades above $x_{\min}$.

$(\alpha, x_{\min})$ in the paper. Because $x_{\min}$ is measured in cases, it

scale of the season is, to that extent, a re-encoding of $S_{t-1}$, which any
model of size already carries. Both readings are correct, and they make different
predictions about the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:prediction}.

\subsection{The fitted law as a risk curve}
\label{sec:riskmodel}

Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed} makes the description operational. A power law with
exponent $\alpha$ and lower bound $x_{\min}$ assigns
$\Pr(S \ge s)=\zeta(\alpha,s)/\zeta(\alpha,x_{\min})$ for every level $s$, so that
the fitted pair is a complete statement about the frequency of extreme seasons. It
requires no fitted case count, no covariate and no training period: it is
available from the current cross-section alone.

Figure~\ref{fig:exceed} confronts the exceedance curve implied by each national
fit with the empirical exceedance frequency of the same cross-section. The
agreement holds over roughly two decades of epidemic size. Numerically, the 16
national fits imply that between 5.6\% and 16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten
times $x_{\min}$ in a given season, and that between 0.3\% and 2.7\% exceed one
hundred times it. These are statements on which a surveillance service can act
and which a subsequent season can falsify.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig09_exceedance}
\caption{The fitted power law as a probabilistic statement about exceedance. For
each national cross-section, curves give
$\Pr(S \ge s)=\zeta(\alpha,s)/\zeta(\alpha,x_{\min})$ evaluated at the fitted

This is the sense in which the description is predictive before any statistical
learning is involved: $\alpha$ is not a summary of central tendency but a summary
of how much of the risk lies in the tail, and it is available for every
municipality even where that municipality has never experienced an extreme
season. The familiar inference, that a divergent second moment renders extreme
seasons unpredictable, is a statement about squared-error loss and not about
information. Prediction of a tail event and prediction of its magnitude are
distinct problems.

\subsection{Predicting the probability of a large season}
\label{sec:classresults}

The exceedance formulation has a direct predictive counterpart: rather than
asking how large the season will be, ask whether it will fall in the upper
quartile of the unit's own history. This is the risk statement itself, and it is
the natural place to look for value in the scaling descriptors, since a tail
exponent is by construction a statement about upper-tail probabilities.

We posed the question at the two levels where it is asked operationally. At state
level (Table~\ref{tab:exceed}) the lagged seasonal descriptors --- which include
the previous season's duration and peak timing as ordinary covariates, alongside
size, $R_0$ and population --- move the Brier score in the expected direction: a
median of $+0.126$, positive in 4 of 5 origins and reaching $+0.212$. The
intervals exclude zero in only 2 of the 5 origins, against the 3 that the rule requires, so the rung fails the rule at state level: each origin is a
bootstrap over 23 to 25 units and the median interval is $0.26$ wide, wider than
the effect it is asked to resolve. Climate adds nothing to it (median $+0.027$) and neither does the scaling block
(median $+0.000$, with no origin in which the block improves by an interval excluding
zero). The per-origin
differentials for M1 against M0 are $-0.064$, $+0.074$, $+0.126$, $+0.212$ and $+0.149$ for 2021 to 2025, negative in the first origin and positive thereafter,
which is the pattern expected of a model that requires history in order to be
trained.

At municipal level the same ladder runs over 3{,}976 municipalities with enough
history for a within-unit threshold, at least five seasons, with between 580 and
1{,}484 units evaluated per origin. Two things change, and only one of them
concerns the scaling descriptors. The first is a correction. With the threshold
taken from each municipality's own seasons, the population-only reference falls to a
median area under the curve of $0.47$ (range $0.454$--$0.522$), against $0.87$ (range
$0.84$--$0.93$) where the threshold is pooled across units, as it must be at state
level: a pooled label is largely a restatement of unit size, whereas the within-unit
label is not. The second is that the lagged block, which the state level could not
resolve, satisfies the rule here. Its Brier differential has a median of $+0.049$, positive and with an
interval excluding zero in 4 of 5 origins, the only contrast in this study to satisfy
the pre-specified rule (Table~\ref{tab:exceed}). Discrimination rises from $0.50$ for
the no-skill reference to $0.58$, $0.63$ and $0.65$ for M1, M2 and M3; M3 is the best
of the three in 3 of 5 origins and gains a median $+0.013$ over M2.

The inversion between the two levels is a statement about precision and not about
epidemiology. The state estimate of the same effect is the larger of the two in
absolute terms, $+0.126$ against $+0.049$, and the less certain by a factor of
eight in interval width, $0.26$ against $0.034$, so that a rule which fails at one
level and passes at the other is failing on sample size: twenty-five units per
origin cannot resolve an effect that 1{,}484 units can. The two levels also differ in
the scope of the label, which is pooled across states but taken within each
municipality, so the state comparison inherits the size ranking described above and is
the weaker of the two tests of epidemic rarity. What the two levels agree on is the
ranking that matters for this paper. The scaling block satisfies the criterion at
neither level: at municipal level it moves in the expected direction in 5 of 5 origins,
by a median of $+0.002$, but its interval excludes zero in only one of them, and at
state level it moves in the expected direction in 1 of 5 and has an interval excluding
zero in 2, both against the block. Whether a municipality will experience an
exceptional season is in part predictable from how large the previous one was; the
exponent does not add to that.

One forecast origin requires comment. In 2024, 69.5\% of the evaluated
municipalities exceeded their own upper quartile, against a training base rate of
26.4\%. The no-skill reference scores a Brier score of $0.40$ there and the fitted rungs about $0.25$, better than the reference but worse than the $0.21$ that a
predictor knowing the season's own frequency would earn. That season was a
national record, and no model trained on earlier seasons anticipated its level.
This is the boundary of the exercise: the classifier ranks units within a season
of familiar magnitude; it does not anticipate a change of regime.

and attribution for the municipal classifier. The calibration panel is the

\subsection{Predicting epidemic size}
\label{sec:prediction}

The harder question is whether the tail helps forecast the magnitude of a season,
which is the quantity against which a health secretariat plans capacity. Here the
answer is negative, and the reason is the coupling measured in
Section~\ref{sec:coupling}.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation_results} reports the ladder over the five origins at state
level. Against calibrated persistence, the lagged-descriptor rung M1 carries
essentially no skill for size (median skill $0.30$, range $-0.06$ to $0.86$, positive in
3 of 5 origins and with an interval excluding zero in 2 of 5), and
successive rungs do not improve on it: M2 has median $0.12$ and M3 $0.15$. The
differential of M3 against M2, the one that isolates
$(\alpha_{t-1}, x_{\min,t-1}, \dot\alpha_{t-1})$ and nothing else, shows no origin in
which the block improves with an interval excluding zero, against the three the rule
requires; its interval excludes zero in 2 of the 5 origins, both against the block,
with a median relative change in pinball loss at $\tau=0.5$ of $-5.2\%$.

\input{generated/tab_ablation}

The block was also expected to be most valuable at high $\tau$. The argument for
$\alpha_{t-1}$ is that it encodes how much of the risk lies in the tail, so that
any gain should concentrate at high $\tau$, and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}b was
constructed to test precisely that. It shows not a degradation but an absence of
signal. At $\tau=0.95$ the median differential is $+4.9\%$, with M3 ahead, and it
is positive in 4 of 5 origins; but the intervals exclude zero in only 2 of 5, and those two point in \emph{opposite} directions, $+32.7\%$ in 2021 and
$-15.4\%$ in 2024. The same holds across the grid: the median of the per-origin
differential moves between $-13.6\%$ and $+7.2\%$ with no trend, and there is no
quantile at which 3 of 5 origins agree in direction with an interval
excluding zero, 0 of 19 quantiles clearing the criterion. This is what the
coupling predicts. Both statistics are functions of the magnitude of the previous
season, so that the block is at best a second and noisier copy of $S_{t-1}$; the
ablation shows that the copy carries no usable increment, which leaves the upper
quantile where it was rather than displacing it in either direction.

Two origins dominate the size target and require separate treatment. The 2024
season was the largest on record and 2025 fell back sharply; persistence
extrapolated the record into 2025 and, in absolute terms, lost there much of what
it had gained in 2024. The pinball losses for size are accordingly an order of
magnitude larger in those two origins, $1.5$ to $1.7\times10^{5}$ cases, than in
the other three, $1.2$ to $3.2\times10^{4}$, so that a pooled mean over origins
would mostly describe how each rung behaves across a change of regime. We
therefore report the median across origins and the sign of the per-origin
differential rather than a pooled average.

That change of regime is the substantive lesson of this subsection, and it is
consistent with the descriptive result rather than in tension with it. If epidemic
size follows $\alpha \approx 2$, the largest season a municipality will experience
lies systematically outside the support of its observed history. A model that
already knows the size of the previous season is therefore approximately correct in
ordinary seasons and wrong by a large factor in the exceptional one, which is
exactly the pattern of the table and of Figure~\ref{fig:predobs}. The estimator
of Section~\ref{sec:regression} determines how that pattern is to be read, not the
pattern itself. Because it can produce a value
beyond its training responses, a large error in a record season is a statement
about the data, and the failure of the size forecast can be read as a finding
rather than as a property of the fit.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the exponent is, and what it is not}

The comparison across five constructions settles a question that the applied
literature has treated as empirical when it is largely definitional. Median
exponents span $1.95$ to $2.08$ across constructions of the same series, the share
of fits favouring a power law ranges from 23\% to 100\%, and a single change of
temporal window moves support from 77\% to 23\% with no transmission parameter
altered. What does not survive is the inference from an exponent to a regime: the
step that treats $\alpha$ as a property of the pathogen or of the place, when it is
a property of the tuple (variable, sampling unit, dimension of pooling, comparison
model).

The same comparison identifies which construction \emph{does} carry a stable tail.
Epidemic size across units within a season is power-law at every construction at
which $x_{\min}$ lies above the bulk: all 16 national cross-sections, 96\% support
across 353 state-year cross-sections, and a lower bound in the hundreds of cases,
two to three orders of magnitude above the body of the distribution. That is not a
fragile result, and it is the one on which the paper builds.

The distinction between this result and the weekly-count fits that collapse to
$x_{\min} \le 5$ is substantive rather than technical. At $x_{\min}=2$ the fitted
law describes counts of one and two cases; at $x_{\min}=730$ it describes the upper
decade of a distribution. Two analyses reported as agreeing on the exponent can be
describing entirely different objects, as the comparison of \lvl{W-mun} and \lvl{S-mun} shows: a
difference of $0.13$ in the exponent and a factor of $150$ in the lower bound.

\subsection{The tail as a risk specification rather than a forecast input}

The most consequential move in this paper is to stop treating $\alpha \approx 2$ as
a verdict on forecastability and to treat it as a probability statement. A power
law assigns $\Pr(S \ge s)$ at every level; what it precludes is that the
\emph{magnitude} of the largest event be estimated from the variance. These are
distinct claims, and conflating them is what produced the intuition that
heavy-tailed epidemics are unforecastable.

Read as a risk specification, the fitted pair performs well and cheaply. Two
parameters reproduce the empirical exceedance frequency over two decades of
epidemic size (Figure~\ref{fig:exceed}), and they imply that between 5.6\% and
16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times $x_{\min}$ in a given season against
0.3--2.7\% exceeding a hundred times it. No covariate, no training period and no
fitted case count is involved: the statement is available from the current
cross-section, for every municipality, including those that have never experienced
an extreme season. For a service deciding how much capacity to hold in reserve,
this is closer to the decision than a point forecast is.

The predictive evaluation confirms the division of labour and locates it more
sharply than a single level could. Where the question is posed as a probability ---
will this municipality exceed its own upper quartile? --- the answer is
affirmative, but only where the panel can resolve it. At municipal level lagged
seasonal descriptors improve the Brier score over calibrated persistence in 4 of 5
origins and are significantly the worse in the fifth, and they raise discrimination
from $0.50$ to $0.58$ in area under the curve, the full ladder reaching $0.65$. At
state level the same rung produces a larger point estimate and fails the rule, on 23
to 25 units per origin and with a label pooled across them. The scaling block, which is
the object of the paper, satisfies the criterion at neither level. Where the question is posed as a magnitude, the answer
is negative, and the failure is orderly rather than unexplained.

\subsection{How the coupling explains both results}

The coupling between $\alpha$ and $x_{\min}$ is the mechanism behind both halves of
the predictive result, and it merits reading as a finding rather than as a caveat.
Within units the two estimates are rank-correlated at $0.64$ (median over 24 states)
and at $0.89$ nationally, whereas a simulation with the true exponent held fixed at
$2.0$ recovers $-0.20$. The association is a property of the data and not of the
estimator.

Descriptively, this makes the pair a compact readout of seasonal magnitude: two
numbers that move with the size of the season, vary far less than the case counts
they summarise (SD $0.150$ against a sampling noise of $0.03$), and
are available for every unit. This is a substantive contribution to the
characterisation of a season, since it reduces a quantity spanning two orders of
magnitude to a stable low-dimensional summary.

Predictively, the same fact accounts for the null. An exponent that co-varies with
the scale of the season is, to that extent, a re-encoding of $S_{t-1}$, which the
model already contains. The ablation measures that redundancy directly: the scaling
block adds nothing to a rung that already knows the previous season's size, in 0 of 5 origins, and at $\tau=0.95$ its differential is $+4.9\%$ in median with
intervals that exclude zero in only 2 of 5 origins and in opposite directions,
so that the design has no power there to sign the effect in either direction. The
redundancy was predictable from the coupling before the ablation was run, and the
ablation confirmed it where the prediction had content: a re-encoding of a variable
already in the model must deliver no incremental information, and none appears at
any quantile of the grid.

The same reasoning explains why the exceedance question behaves differently.
Asking whether a season will be large is a question about upper-tail probability,
and the coefficient on the lagged descriptors is doing the work there; adding a
transformation of the same lagged quantity cannot improve it, which is why the
Brier differentials of the scaling block sit near zero with no interval excluding
it. The information the exponent carries is real, and it is already contained in
the lagged count.

\subsection{Size as the hard target, consistently with the description}

Nothing in this paper shows that epidemic size can be forecast well. Against
calibrated persistence no rung achieves skill in more than 3 of 5 origins,
and 2 of the 5 origins dominate the loss because they straddle a change of
regime: 2024 was the largest season on record and 2025 fell back sharply.

The failure is not an artefact of the estimator. The model is a
spline-basis quantile regression, which by construction continues the boundary
trend beyond the largest response on which it was trained
(Figure~\ref{fig:predobs}), so that a record season is a statement about the data
rather than a property of the estimator. The quantile regression at $\tau=0.95$
represents the upper region instead of interpolating within it, and the forecast
still fails in 2024: knowledge of the previous season's size and duration, of its
peak week, of the climate covariates and of the scaling descriptors does not order
five origins at 23 to 25 units each.

The defensible summary is therefore a division of labour rather than a verdict. The
tail does not indicate how large the next season will be; it indicates how likely an
extreme season is, and does so with two parameters and no training data. These are
different products for different decisions, and the second is the one a surveillance
service can use before the season begins.

\subsection{A reporting convention}

The practical consequence of the descriptive half is a reporting convention, and it
is inexpensive to adopt. An exponent should travel with the lower bound that
produced it, the construction of the sample, the number of observations and the
alternative model against which it was tested. Of these, $x_{\min}$ is the most
informative and the most frequently omitted, because it separates two analyses that
look identical in a table of results. The diagnostic is easy to apply and difficult
to manipulate, since $x_{\min}$ is selected by the same goodness-of-fit criterion
that selects the model and cannot be tuned to produce a desired $\alpha$. We would
argue further that an $\alpha$ reported without $x_{\min}$ should be treated as an
incomplete statistic, in the same way that a correlation is incomplete without its
sample size.

We would add a second convention, specific to the argument tested here. A claim that
a scaling description improves forecasts is meaningful only against a specified
benchmark, on a proper scoring rule, out of sample, with the scaling block isolated
from the blocks to which it is added, and with every channel of leakage closed. The
redundancy documented here would be invisible under a pooled coefficient of
determination without a benchmark, since between-unit differences are reproduced
trivially by persistence.

\subsection{Relation to operational forecast systems}

Operational arbovirus platforms --- InfoDengue and Mosqlimate in Brazil, and the
national systems reviewed elsewhere --- already produce weekly municipal nowcasts
and short-horizon forecasts \citep{araujo2025large}. Alongside them, multivariate
machine-learning models have been fitted to municipal dengue series in Brazil,
comparing recurrent networks with random forest regression
\citep{mussumeci2020lstm}. The operational systems answer the question that this
paper finds difficult: what is happening now, and what will happen in the coming
weeks. The scaling description answers a question that they largely do not:
given the history of a municipality, how often should its cross-section produce a
season of a given size, expressed as a probability with two parameters. The two are
complementary, and the second is inexpensive enough to be computed for every
municipality in the country from a single cross-section.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound these conclusions.

\emph{The scaling block does not improve the size forecast.} This is reported in the
Results rather than only here because it is a measured outcome, but it is the
principal negative result of the paper and should accompany any use of it. Against a
benchmark that already carries the lagged case count, the block of
$(\alpha_{t-1}, x_{\min,t-1}, \dot\alpha_{t-1})$ fails the pre-specified test on size
in 0 of 5 origins. At the 95th percentile its differential is $+4.9\%$ in
median, with intervals excluding zero in only 2 of 5 origins and in opposite
directions, so that the design cannot sign the effect there: with 23 to 25 units per
origin the test is underpowered at the extreme quantile, and we report the absence of
a measured gain rather than a measured deterioration. The mechanism is the coupling
documented in Section~\ref{sec:coupling}. A rung containing the scaling descriptors
\emph{alone}, without the lagged descriptors, would answer a different and
interesting question --- whether three power-law parameters can substitute for the
full set of covariates --- and is not reported here.

\emph{The municipal classifier is a ranking device, not a level forecast.} With the
exceedance label referred to each municipality's own history, the population-only
reference falls to a median area under the curve of $0.47$, so that the task no longer
measures unit size, and the lagged block satisfies the pre-specified incremental test
at that level. What bounds the claim is the purpose of the classifier: discrimination
reaches a median of $0.65$ only with the full ladder, against $0.58$ for the rung that
satisfies the rule, the scaling block adds a median $+0.002$ in Brier score,
and the 2024 origin, in which 69.5\% of units exceeded their own upper quartile
against a training base rate of 26.4\%, defeats every rung. The classifier orders
municipalities within a season of familiar magnitude. It does not anticipate a change
of regime, and in a record season its probabilities should not be read as levels.

\emph{The case series are nowcast-adjusted.} The analysed variable is the estimated
case count produced by the nowcasting model of the platform, not the raw notification
count. This is the appropriate series for a retrospective description, because raw
notifications are incomplete in recent weeks, but the upper tail inherits whatever
smoothing the nowcast applies. If the nowcast shrinks extreme values, the fitted
exponents are biased towards the bulk, and the size distribution reported here is
somewhat less heavy than the notification distribution would be.

\emph{The state level is an arithmetic aggregate.} Where duration and peak timing
enter as covariates, the state values are the mean of municipal durations and the
median of municipal peaks. Neither is the duration or the peak of the aggregate
epidemic, which is the quantity a state health secretariat actually experiences. The
state-level results should be read as properties of the distribution of municipalities
within a state, not as properties of the epidemic of the state.

\emph{The cross-sections are short.} The national constructions rest on 16 annual
fits and the state constructions on three to 16 seasons per state, so that the
correlations reported in Section~\ref{sec:coupling} are estimated on small samples
even where the underlying cross-sections contain thousands of municipalities. The
within-state estimates are more robust than the national ones because they pool over
24 states.

\emph{Extrapolation rests on an assumption rather than on data.} The primary
estimator continues the boundary trend of the spline basis linearly, which is what
allows it to name a season larger than any in its training window; but the value it
produces in that region is an extrapolation of a local slope, and it is only as
reliable as the assumption that the slope holds. In a simulation constructed at the
scale of this panel, with a season twice the previous one and training seasons of 10
to 1{,}000 cases, the model recovered the doubling to within 0.1\% at five times the
largest value in its training window and within 0.2\% at twenty times. This
establishes that the estimator recovers the relationship when the relationship is of
the assumed form; it does not establish that the relationship holds for dengue. The
defensible reading is that the estimator removes the ceiling on the magnitude the
forecast can take, so that a large error in a record season can be attributed to the
data, and that it does not remove the risk of a large error.

\emph{The classification threshold is a choice.} Exceeding the 75th percentile of a
unit's own history defines a large season; at the 90th percentile the positive class
becomes small enough that the balanced accuracy of any classifier falls and the
comparison between rungs loses power. The threshold is therefore a design parameter,
fixed before the evaluation and reported with it.

\emph{The exponent cannot exceed three.} The estimator bounds $\alpha$ above by $3$,
so that a steeper tail is reported as $3$ rather than as its unconstrained maximum.
This is immaterial here: no national or state-year size fit approaches the bound, the
largest values being $2.94$ at state level and $2.25$ nationally, and it is
reached only by 12 of the 48{,}937 weekly fits, or $0.02\%$. Every exponent reported
in this paper is an unconstrained maximum.

\emph{Predictors are ecological and coarse.} No serotype composition, no
seroprevalence and no vector index enters the model, although all three are plausible
determinants of season size. None of the three is observable with the data at
hand: the attack ratio can be recovered from aggregated incidence only under strong
assumptions about the initial susceptible fraction, and serological surveys are what
would be needed to characterise serotype-specific immunity \citep{coelho2015attack};
where susceptibility has been built into municipal dengue forecasts it has improved
their weather-based predictions, which is reason to expect its omission to matter
here \citep{mcgough2021dynamic}. To the extent that these are stable within a unit they
are absorbed by unit-level effects; to the extent that they vary by season they
constitute unmodelled heterogeneity that the scaling block may be inadvertently
representing. This is a tenable explanation for the null reported here, and it is the
first that we would test.

\subsection{Implications for surveillance practice}

Three practices follow, none of them costly.

First, report the tuple. An exponent accompanied by its construction, its $x_{\min}$,
its sample size and its comparison model can be assessed by a reader; an exponent
alone cannot, and the five constructions examined here show that the same exponent can
carry opposite meanings.

Second, use the exceedance curve. Two parameters fitted to the current cross-section
give the probability that a municipality will exceed any level, and the national fits
reproduce the observed frequency of large seasons over two decades. This is an
inexpensive input to planning that requires no model training and no history beyond
the current season.

Third, do not translate a heavy tail into a claim of unpredictability without
specifying the loss function. A distribution without a finite variance constrains
squared-error point forecasts and leaves probabilistic exceedance statements intact,
and the exceedance statement is usually the one a health service requires.

\section{Conclusion}

The distribution of dengue epidemic size in Brazil is heavy-tailed with an exponent
close to two, and this is among the few robust facts in the applied literature on
epidemic scaling: 16 of 16 national cross-sections, 96\% of 353 state-year
cross-sections, and a lower bound two to three orders of magnitude above the bulk.
Which of these statements a study reports, and whether it reports a power law or an
exponential at all, is determined by the construction it imposes on the data; that
heterogeneity is methodological before it is biological.

The consequence for forecasting is not the one usually drawn. Because
$\alpha \approx 2$ fixes the entire exceedance curve, the fitted pair is a
two-parameter risk specification, and it is well calibrated against the empirical
frequency of large seasons over two decades of epidemic size. Posed as a probability,
the question of whether a municipality will exceed its own upper quartile is
tractable: lagged seasonal descriptors improve the Brier score over calibrated
persistence in 4 of 5 municipal origins, the only contrast here to satisfy the
pre-specified rule, and raise discrimination from $0.50$ to $0.58$ in area under the
curve, the full ladder reaching $0.65$, whereas at state level the same contrast
cannot be resolved on 25 units. Posed as a magnitude, it is not tractable: no rung
achieves skill for size, and the scaling block specifically adds nothing, in 0 of 5
origins, its differential at the 95th percentile being $+4.9\%$ in median with
intervals that exclude zero in only 2 of 5 origins.

That last result is not an accident of the data. The exponent co-varies with the
lower bound, and hence with the scale of the season, at $0.89$ nationally and $0.64$
within states, in a manner that simulation does not reproduce. The pair is therefore
a genuine low-variance summary of how large a season was, and for that same reason a
redundant input to any model that already knows how large the previous one was. The
redundancy was predicted from the coupling before the ablation was run, and the
ablation confirmed it.

What generalises beyond this dataset is the shape of the question. A heavy tail does
not render epidemics unpredictable; it bounds how well the magnitude of the largest
event can be estimated from second moments, and it leaves the probability of exceeding
any level fully specified. The exponent is worth reporting. It is worth reporting as
a risk curve, with its lower bound, and not as a predictor.
