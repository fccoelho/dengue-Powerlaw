\begin{abstract}
Power-law scaling is routinely reported for infectious disease incidence, and
dengue in Brazil is a standard example: heavy-tailed distributions of case
counts are invoked to argue that extreme epidemics are structurally
unpredictable, and therefore that anticipating them is a matter of luck rather
than skill. We test both halves of that argument, on the same data, at once.
Using weekly dengue surveillance data spanning January 2010 to January 2026
for the 5{,}571 Brazilian municipalities, processed through the
Mosqlimate/Infodengue platform, we fitted discrete power laws by maximum
likelihood with Kolmogorov--Smirnov $x_{\min}$ selection at seven nested
aggregation levels, compared each fit against an exponential alternative by a
likelihood-ratio test, and then asked whether the resulting scaling
descriptors add out-of-sample predictive value beyond lagged case counts,
demographics and climate.

The descriptive results split sharply. For the cross-sectional distribution of
\emph{epidemic size} across municipalities within a year, the power law is
preferred in 100\% of the 15 national fits (all significant at $p<0.05$;
$\alpha \approx 2.0$) and in 96\% of 330 state-year fits, with $x_{\min}$ in
the hundreds of cases. For the distribution of \emph{epidemic duration}, the
power law is rejected in all 15 national fits (and in 95\% of the state-year
fits) in favour of an exponential, with $\alpha$ piling up at
$2.90$--$2.96$. For weekly case counts, the power law appears preferred in
75\% of city-level series but $x_{\min} \le 5$ in 77\% of them, meaning that
the fitted object is the bulk of the distribution rather than a tail.

The predictive half turns the description into a testable one. A power law
with $\alpha\approx2$ fixes the whole exceedance curve, so the fitted pair
$(\alpha, x_{\min})$ is a two-parameter statement about how often extreme
seasons occur --- and, unlike the case counts themselves, the exponent is
empirically stable relative to their two-order-of-magnitude variation, even
though it is not independent of the scale of the season: within units,
$\alpha$ and $x_{\min}$ are strongly rank-correlated, so a scaling block may
be re-encoding the lagged case count rather than adding to it. We therefore use
the lagged scaling descriptors as features in (i) random-forest and
gradient-boosted quantile regressions of epidemic size, duration and peak
timing, and (ii) a classifier of whether a municipality will exceed its
historical 75th percentile. Because $\alpha\approx2$ makes squared-error
losses dominated by a few tail events, evaluation is on proper scoring rules
--- pinball loss and the Brier score --- over a rolling-origin design with
five forecast origins, and a four-rung ablation ladder isolates the
incremental contribution of the scaling block from that of lagged metrics,
population and climate.

We conclude that the heavy-tail literature on dengue conflates at least three
distinct random variables, that scale claims are only interpretable where
$x_{\min}$ lies far above the bulk, and that reporting $\alpha$ without the
$(\text{unit},\ x_{\min},\ \text{comparison model})$ tuple is uninformative
--- descriptive or predictive. A heavy tail is not, by itself, evidence that
epidemics cannot be anticipated: it bounds how well the largest event's
magnitude can be estimated from second moments, and leaves the probability of
exceeding any level fully specified.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Understanding the dynamics of infectious disease epidemics has led to a very
prolific literature, both in terms of mechanistic mathematical models and of
statistical models \citep{siettos2013mathematical}. The former attempt to
capture the causal chain of events involved in disease transmission, the
latter try to make sense of a growing collection of data about infectious
disease incidence.

Among the most salient results of the mathematical modelling of infectious
diseases was the definition of the basic reproductive number, $R_0$, which
determines the most important characteristics of epidemics such as their
spreading speed and the epidemic threshold, also known as the herd immunity
threshold. $R_0$ appears in the equations governing prevalence growth as an
exponent determining the initial exponential growth of an epidemic, and it
defines an important bifurcation point at $R_0=1$ linked to the existence and
stability of equilibria in epidemic models.

On the statistical side, the largest portion of the literature deals with
statistical modelling of incidence data, i.e.\ the number of cases accumulated
over time and space or studied as a stochastic process, such as a Poisson or
negative binomial process \citep{becker1999statistical, li2012log}. The
central difficulty is overdispersion: real surveillance counts produce far more
extreme observations than any tractable count model comfortably allows.

A recurrent claim is that this overdispersion is not merely large but
\emph{scale-free}, i.e.\ that incidence follows a power law
\citep{rhodes1997critical, clauset2009power, neipel2020power}. The attraction of
this claim is that the very heavy tail implies extreme values that most models
fail to predict --- and those are precisely the events that cause the most
disease and the largest economic burden. If true, this would have direct
consequences for forecasting and for resource allocation.

The problem is that the empirical record is inconsistent. Reported exponents
for dengue range widely, some studies find exponential rather than power-law
behaviour, and the presence of a heavy tail appears to depend on the study
period, the spatial unit and the outcome variable. Previous work has usually
attributed this heterogeneity to biology or geography: different places, or
different years, are assumed to be governed by different regimes.

Here we test a simpler and more uncomfortable explanation: that the
heterogeneity is largely produced by the analyst. Any epidemiological
time series can be cut into distributions in several legitimate ways --- the
weekly counts of one city, the annual totals of one city, the set of epidemic
sizes across cities in one year, the set of epidemic durations --- and these
are different random variables with different supports and different tail
behaviour. If the verdict changes as the construction changes, then a
substantial part of the published disagreement is methodological rather than
biological.

\paragraph{Why the description matters for forecasting.}
The dispute would be of limited practical consequence if it stayed on the
descriptive side. It does not. The heavy-tail claim is routinely used as an
argument about forecastability: if epidemic sizes are drawn from a power law
with exponent near 2, then the variance of the size distribution is dominated
by its extreme upper tail, every squared-error forecast metric is decided by a
handful of observations, and the honest conclusion is that point forecasts of
epidemic size are unreliable. That inference is correct as far as it goes, but
it says nothing about whether \emph{probabilistic} forecasts are possible. A
power law is a complete generative statement: it assigns a probability to
every exceedance level, so the pair $(\alpha, x_{\min})$ summarises the whole
risk curve of a municipality, and a stable $\alpha$ is a low-variance quantity
in a setting where the observables are wildly volatile. Whether that summary
carries information not already present in the lagged case counts is an
empirical question, and it is the question the second half of this paper puts
to the test: we first establish the scaling description, then submit it to an
out-of-sample predictive evaluation in which it can either earn its place
among the predictors or be shown to be redundant.

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper makes four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A systematic seven-level comparison on one dataset.} We fit the
same estimator to the same underlying surveillance data under seven nested
aggregation levels (Table~\ref{tab:levels}), so that any difference in the
inferred regime is attributable to the construction and not to differing data
sources, periods or software.

\item \textbf{An identification diagnostic that separates signal from
artefact.} We show that the reported preference for a power law is only
interpretable when the estimated lower bound $x_{\min}$ lies well above the
bulk of the data. Where $x_{\min}$ collapses to 1--5 cases, the fit describes
the entire distribution and the ``tail'' language is vacuous. We report
$x_{\min}$ alongside every exponent and use it to classify each of the 47{,}494
fits.

\item \textbf{A resolved claim about dengue.} Epidemic \emph{size} is
heavy-tailed with $\alpha\approx 2$ and is stable across states and years;
epidemic \emph{duration} is not heavy-tailed and is better described by an
exponential. The two variables are frequently conflated in the literature,
which explains a large part of the apparent contradiction.

\item \textbf{A predictive test of the scaling description.} We do not stop at
the exponent. Treating $(\alpha,x_{\min})$ and the historical trend of
$\alpha$ as lagged features, we fit regression and classification models for
epidemic size, duration and peak timing under a rolling-origin design, and use
a four-rung ablation ladder to measure whether the scaling block adds
out-of-sample skill over lagged counts, population and climate --- and at
which aggregation level it does so. This converts a descriptive controversy
into a falsifiable claim about forecast skill.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data sources}

Weekly dengue case counts were obtained from the InfoDengue/Mosqlimate
platform \citep{araujo2025large} for the period from 1 January 2010 to 22
January 2026 (the extraction date), for all Brazilian municipalities with
available data. The
variable used is the nowcast-adjusted estimated number of cases
(\texttt{casos\_est}), resampled to epidemiological weeks ending on Sunday
(\texttt{W-SUN}); zero-case weeks are retained in the series but excluded from
distributional fits, since the power-law family is defined on positive
support.

Annual epidemic descriptors were obtained from the EpiScanner module of the
same platform, which partitions each municipal series into epidemic episodes
and reports, for each episode, its total size (\emph{total cases}) and its
duration in weeks (\emph{ep\_dur}). Annual cross-sections are available for
the 15 epidemic years 2011--2025; the pooled all-years fit is stored
separately (with a sentinel \texttt{year = 0}) and is excluded from all year
counts. This yields, for each state-year and for
each national year, a cross-section of municipal epidemics.

Municipal population figures were taken from the estimates distributed with
the platform (\texttt{city\_stats} table; 5{,}571 municipalities, one with a
zero entry excluded). Population enters no power-law fit; it is used to compute
the descriptive correlations of Section~\ref{sec:verdicts} and, as
$\log_{10}$ population, as one predictor in the forecasting models of
Section~\ref{sec:targets}.

\subsection{Discrete power-law estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

For each sample we fit a discrete power law by maximum likelihood following
\citet{clauset2009power}. The fitted density for integer $x \ge x_{\min}$ is
%
\begin{equation}
p(x) = \frac{x^{-\alpha}}{\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})},
\end{equation}
%
where $\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})$ is the generalised (Hurwitz) zeta function,
which normalises the discrete mass. The exponent $\alpha$ is estimated by
numerical maximisation of the likelihood, and the lower bound $x_{\min}$ is
selected jointly by minimising the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the
empirical and fitted cumulative distributions over all candidate values of
$x_{\min}$. Because the data are counts, the discrete formulation is used
throughout (\texttt{discrete=True}); the continuous approximation is not
employed at any level.

One property of the estimator has to be stated because it bears on the duration
results. The numerical fit constrains $\alpha$ to $(1,3]$: the upper limit of
the optimiser's default parameter range is $3$, so an estimate cannot exceed it
and a sample whose likelihood is maximised beyond that value returns $\alpha=3$
rather than its unconstrained maximum. The bound is immaterial where the fitted
exponents lie well inside the range. At the cross-sectional size levels the
largest value is $2.99$ (L4) and $2.27$ (L6); at the two weekly levels it is
reached by 2 and 5 fits respectively, under $0.05\%$ of each. It binds
materially only for durations, where the upper quartile of the 330 state-year
fits lies above $2.92$ and 11 of them ($3.3\%$) sit at the ceiling. Those fits
should be read as \emph{at least} $3$, not as $3$, and a genuinely scale-free
body would not systematically exhaust the upper bound of the estimator.

The choice of $x_{\min}$ is not a nuisance parameter. It determines which
fraction of the data the model actually describes, and therefore whether the
fitted exponent refers to a tail at all. We therefore retain $x_{\min}$ for
every fit and report its median and the proportion of fits with
$x_{\min}\le 5$ in each aggregation level.

\subsection{Model comparison}

A power law can always be fitted; the question is whether it describes the
data better than a plausible alternative. We compare each power-law fit
against an exponential distribution, which is the natural null for
epidemiological counts because it corresponds to a constant hazard of
termination and produces a light tail. The comparison follows the
likelihood-ratio procedure of \citet{clauset2009power}, as implemented in the
\texttt{powerlaw} package with \texttt{normalized\_ratio=True}.
%
\begin{equation}
R = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\ln p_{\mathrm{PL}}(x_i) - \ln p_{\mathrm{exp}}(x_i)\right],
\end{equation}
%
normalised so that its magnitude is comparable across samples of different
size. The sign of $R$ indicates the preferred model ($R>0$ favours the power
law, $R<0$ favours the exponential) and its associated $p$-value quantifies
whether the sign is distinguishable from sampling noise.

We adopt a deliberately conservative reporting convention. A level is
described as \emph{power-law supported} only when $R>0$ and $p<0.05$; as
\emph{exponential supported} only when $R<0$ and $p<0.05$; and as
\emph{inconclusive} otherwise. This prevents the common error of counting a
positive but non-significant $R$ as evidence for scaling.

\subsection{Identification diagnostic}

We flag a fit as \emph{tail-informative} when $x_{\min}$ exceeds the bulk of
the observed distribution, operationally $x_{\min}>5$ cases for weekly counts
and $x_{\min}$ above the median observed value for the aggregate levels. Where
$x_{\min}\le 5$, the fitted power law is being asked to describe the entire
empirical distribution, including the small-count regime where the discrete
power law is not a plausible generative model (case counts of 1 or 2 arise
from reporting mechanics, not from scale-free branching). Such fits are
retained in the database but are excluded from substantive interpretation, and
we report the proportion explicitly.

\subsection{From description to prediction}
\label{sec:bridge}

A fitted power law is not merely a description of a histogram; it is a
statement about exceedance probabilities. For integer $s \ge x_{\min}$,
%
\begin{equation}
\Pr(S \ge s) \;=\; \frac{\zeta(\alpha,s)}{\zeta(\alpha,x_{\min})},
\label{eq:exceed}
\end{equation}
%
so a single pair $(\alpha,x_{\min})$ parameterises the probability that a
municipality experiences an epidemic of at least any given size. Four
consequences of Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed} shape the forecasting design.

First, the exponent summarises \emph{risk} rather than \emph{activity}. Two
municipalities with the same expected annual incidence but different $\alpha$
face different probabilities of a catastrophic season, and that difference is
invisible to the mean. The fitted models quantify the claim rather than merely
asserting it: using the 15 national size fits, they imply that between 5.4\%
and 16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times $x_{\min}$ in a given year, and
between 0.3\% and 2.7\% exceed one hundred times $x_{\min}$. Those are
falsifiable implications of the description, and Figure~\ref{fig:exceed}
confronts them with the empirical exceedance frequencies.

Second, $\alpha$ is a low-variance quantity \emph{relative to its target}.
Across the national cross-sections it takes values between 1.78 and 2.27, and it
is essentially unrelated to city size ($r=-0.065$). Annual case counts over the
same period vary by two orders of magnitude. The dispersion of $\hat\alpha$ is
not zero --- it exceeds sampling noise by about a factor of five in standard
deviation, and it co-varies with the selected $x_{\min}$
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}) --- but it is small along precisely the dimension
in which the target is volatile. That is the specific reason to carry $\alpha$
into the predictive models rather than treating it as a descriptive curiosity,
and the same coupling is the reason the ablation, not the correlation, has to
decide the question.

Third, $\alpha\approx2$ has a direct methodological consequence for
evaluation. The second moment of a power law with $\alpha\le 2$ does not exist
in the continuous limit, and at $\alpha\approx2$ the sample mean is unstable,
so squared-error losses are decided by the few largest outbreaks. A model that
is good everywhere except in the tail can therefore look excellent or terrible
depending on which years fall in the test set. We consequently evaluate on
quantile (pinball) losses and on the classification of tail events, and treat
RMSE as a secondary, tail-dominated diagnostic.

Finally, the predictive hypothesis is stated in a form that can fail. If
$(\alpha,x_{\min})$ carries information about the coming season beyond what
lagged counts already encode, a model containing them should beat an otherwise
identical model without them, out of sample. If instead the exponent is close
to a deterministic function of the lower moments of the same series, the two
models should be indistinguishable --- and the descriptive result would then
carry no forecasting content. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} specifies the
comparison that decides between these outcomes, and
Section~\ref{sec:leakage} lists the three ways in which such a comparison could
be faked and how each is prevented.

\subsection{Predictive targets and feature construction}
\label{sec:targets}

The forecasting problem is posed at the level of an administrative unit
(municipality or state) and a target year $t$: every predictor is measured in
year $t-1$ or earlier, and the target is a property of the epidemic season of
year $t$. Three targets are modelled, corresponding to the descriptors that
surveillance services actually act on: epidemic size $S_t$ (total cases in the
annual episode), epidemic duration $D_t$ (weeks), and peak timing $P_t$ (week
of the seasonal maximum).

Predictors fall into six blocks, listed in Table~\ref{tab:features}: lagged
epidemic metrics; the scaling descriptors $(\alpha_{S,t-1},
x_{\min,S,t-1})$ from the power-law fit to the previous season; the historical
trend $\dot\alpha_{t-1}$, defined as the ordinary least-squares slope of
$\alpha_S$ over all years strictly before $t$ and set to zero where fewer than
two such years exist; the epidemiological driver $R_{0,t-1}$ reported by the
EpiScanner decomposition; $\log_{10}$ population; and eight quarterly climate
aggregates from year $t-1$, namely mean minimum temperature and mean minimum
humidity in each of Q1--Q4. Units lacking the mandatory lags (no previous
season) are dropped rather than imputed; the optional $\dot\alpha$ is set to
zero when undefined.

\subsection{Regression models}
\label{sec:regression}

Epidemic size is modelled on the log scale, $\log(1+S_t)$, because the target
spans four orders of magnitude and a model fitted on the raw scale would in
practice be fitted to the largest municipalities only; predictions are
returned to the case scale with $\exp(\cdot)-1$. That back-transformation is
biased downwards by Jensen's inequality, so a multiplicative factor
$c=\overline{S}_{\text{train}}\big/\overline{(\exp\hat y-1)}_{\text{train}}$ is
estimated on the training data and applied to the point predictions. The
correction is a mean-matching device, not a distributional one, and it is
deliberately not applied to the quantile models, whose losses are computed on
the back-transformed quantiles themselves. Duration and peak week are modelled
on their natural scale.

Three estimators are used. (i) A random forest of 150 bootstrapped trees
\citep{breiman2001random}, which additionally provides an out-of-bag $R^2$
reported as an in-sample fit diagnostic. (ii) A LightGBM gradient-boosted
model with quantile objective at level $\tau$
\citep{ke2017lightgbm, koenker1978regression}, with 300 trees, learning rate
0.05, 16 leaves and a minimum of 5 observations per leaf. (iii) A histogram
gradient-boosting model, also with quantile loss, 300 iterations, learning rate
0.05 and maximum depth 5. The quantile models are fitted on the grid
$\tau\in\{0.05,0.10,\dots,0.95\}$, which yields a full predictive distribution
per unit and year rather than a point forecast. All stochastic components use
\texttt{random\_state=42}.

\subsection{Classification of large epidemics}
\label{sec:classifier}

The second predictive task is posed as a decision problem rather than an
estimation problem: will unit $i$ experience, in year $t$, a season larger than
the $p$-th percentile of its own historical distribution? The target is
%
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,t} \;=\; \mathbb{I}\!\left[\,S_{i,t} > Q_p\big(\{S_{i,k}\}_{k<t}\big)\,\right],
\end{equation}
%
with $p=0.75$ by default. The threshold is a \emph{within-fold} quantity: it is
recomputed from the training years of each fold and each split, never from the
full sample, so that the label of a test observation cannot depend on
information unavailable to the model. The same discipline governs the
class-imbalance weight, set to the negative-to-positive ratio of the training
fold.

Two gradient-boosted classifiers are used, XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost} and
LightGBM, with imbalance handled by \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} and
\texttt{is\_unbalance} respectively. Validation is an expanding-window split
defined on the year index. Fold $j$ trains on every unit-year whose year is
earlier than $v_j$ and validates on year $v_j$ alone, where the $v_j$ are the
$k=\min\{5,\,n_{\text{years}}-2\}$ most recent training years: a fold exists
only once at least two earlier years are available, and the number of folds is
capped at five. Because the folds are a function of the year vector and not of
row position, they are invariant to the order of the rows in the frame. Within
each fold the percentile threshold that defines the label, the class-imbalance
weight and the median imputer are all recomputed from that fold's training
years, so no quantity used to label or to weight a validation year can have
been influenced by it; a per-fold audit table (training window, held-out year,
fold sizes, threshold, weight) is retained with the results.

This distinction is not cosmetic, and it is the one step of this pipeline whose
failure is silent. An implementation that applies a contiguous-index split to a
frame sorted by unit rather than by year produces a grouped-by-municipality
split: each fold trains on some municipalities over the whole calendar and
validates on others over the same calendar, so validation contains no year that
is absent from training. On the training panel used here --- 32{,}824 rows,
4{,}725 municipalities, 2010--2023 --- all five folds of such a split train on
2010--2023 and validate on 2010--2023, and 100\% of validation rows carry a
year already present in training. The quantity being estimated is then
transportability to new municipalities, not temporal generalisation, and the
estimate depends on an undeclared sort order: merely reordering the rows of a
synthetic benchmark moved its cross-validated AUC from 0.813 to 0.835, whereas
the year-indexed split returns the same folds and the same AUC under every
permutation of the rows. The trap is narrower but not absent when the frame
happens to be year-sorted, because contiguous blocks then still cut through
calendar years. The splitter therefore takes the year vector explicitly, and
\texttt{assert\_forward\_in\_time} raises rather than warns when any
validation year is not strictly later than the training years of its fold. The decision threshold applied to the predicted
probability is chosen by Youden's $J$ statistic \citep{youden1950index},
computed on the out-of-fold predictions rather than on the training fit, and
then held fixed for the test set. We report the area under the ROC curve, the
area under the precision--recall curve (the more informative summary under
class imbalance), the Brier score \citep{gneiting2007strictly}, the $F_1$ score
at the Youden threshold and a calibration curve, and we test differences in AUC
between nested feature sets by the method of \citet{delong1988comparing}.
Feature attributions are obtained from SHAP values \citep{lundberg2017unified}.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and ablation ladder}
\label{sec:ablation}

A single temporal split cannot distinguish a feature that helps from a feature
that happened to help once. We therefore adopt a rolling-origin design: for
each forecast origin $y\in\{2021,\dots,2025\}$ the model is trained on all
observations with year $<y$ and evaluated on year $y$, and the per-origin
losses are pooled. This gives five honest out-of-sample years and allows skill
to be read as a function of the history available to the model.

Within each origin, the incremental value of the scaling description is
isolated by the ablation ladder of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. Each rung adds one
block of predictors to the previous one, so a contrast between consecutive
rungs is attributable to the block that was added. The rung of substantive
interest is M2 versus M3, which differ \emph{only} in the presence of
$(\alpha_{S,t-1},x_{\min,S,t-1},\dot\alpha_{t-1})$.

M0 is deliberately naive, because a benchmark that is already sophisticated
manufactures skill for whatever is added to it. Skill is reported as
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SS} \;=\; 1-\frac{L(\text{model})}{L(\mathrm{M0})},
\end{equation}
%
on the primary loss of each task --- pinball loss at $\tau=0.5$ for size, and
the Brier score for the classifier, both proper scoring rules
\citep{gneiting2007strictly}. Secondary losses are MAE and RMSE of the point
forecasts, the mean absolute scaled error of \citet{hyndman2006another} to make
errors comparable across units of very different size, and the pinball loss at
each $\tau$ on the grid.

Because units differ by orders of magnitude in scale, all model-versus-model
comparisons are made on paired loss differentials computed within the same unit
and year; their uncertainty is estimated by a bootstrap that resamples
\emph{units} rather than observations, preserving the within-unit correlation
across years, with 2{,}000 replicates. For the pooled comparison across origins
we additionally report the Diebold--Mariano statistic
\citep{diebold1995comparing} with a Newey--West correction, since the loss
series are serially correlated by construction.

The decision rule is fixed in advance. The scaling block is judged to add
predictive value only if M3 improves on M2 on the primary loss with a 95\%
bootstrap interval for the paired difference that excludes zero, in the same
direction, for at least three of the five forecast origins. This is stricter
than a single significant pooled test, and deliberately so: a pooled test can
be carried by one anomalous season, which is exactly the failure mode that a
heavy-tailed target invites.

\subsection{Leakage controls}
\label{sec:leakage}

Four sources of leakage are structurally possible in this design, and each is
closed explicitly.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Contemporaneous scaling descriptors.} $\alpha_{S,t}$ is estimated
from the weekly counts of season $t$, which are part of the target. Only
$\alpha_{S,t-1}$ enters as a predictor, and $\dot\alpha_{t-1}$ is computed
exclusively from years strictly before $t$.

\item \textbf{The classification threshold.} $Q_p$ is computed inside each fold
from training years only. Computing it once on the full sample would leak the
distribution of the test years into the labels of the training years, inflating
apparent skill by construction.

\item \textbf{Cross-sectional information.} The cross-section of municipalities
within year $t$ is an outcome of season $t$ and is therefore never a predictor;
the scaling descriptors always come from the previous season's cross-section.

\item \textbf{The validation split itself.} Folds are defined on the year index
(Section~\ref{sec:classifier}), so no fold trains on a year at or after the
year it validates on. This is the only one of the four channels that fails
silently in a plausible implementation --- every number still looks like a
respectable cross-validation score --- which is why it is asserted in code
rather than merely documented.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Software and reproducibility}

All fits are stored in a single SQLite database
(\texttt{powerlaw\_results.db}) committed to the project repository, together
with the fitting code (\texttt{fitpl.py}) and the cached parquet extracts of
every model result. Every number in this manuscript is derived from that
database by the queries in Appendix~\ref{app:verify}; no statistic is
transcribed by hand. The full pipeline is therefore re-runnable, and a reader
can confirm or contradict each figure with a single SQL statement.

\subsection{Descriptive associations}

The municipal exponent is essentially unrelated to city size:
$\mathrm{corr}(\alpha, \log_{10}\text{population}) = -0.065$ (Spearman
$-0.072$; $n=5{,}244$). The association with incidence is weak and negative
(Pearson $-0.136$, Spearman $-0.248$; $n=4{,}663$). We therefore find no
support for the view that larger or more heavily burdened municipalities sit
in a different scaling regime. The exponent is a property of the aggregation
level and, within a unit, of the scale of the season
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}), not of the municipality. Figure~\ref{fig:stability} shows both the stability and
the absence of association.

Three features of the table matter. The surveillance association is strong at
national level and strong \emph{within} states over time --- positive in 88\% of
the 24 states with at least six years of data, and significantly positive in
67\% --- but weak between states. The coupling is therefore a within-unit,
over-time relationship rather than a contrast between places. Second, the control
recovers no such association, so the estimator does not manufacture it. Third,
the control places the sampling dispersion of $\hat\alpha$ at $0.03$ (40
replicates, samples of 800--4{,}000 points, true exponent fixed at $2.0$),
against an observed standard deviation of $0.154$ across the 15 national fits:
the year-to-year movement in $\alpha$ is real, roughly five times the sampling
noise in standard deviation, even though it remains small next to the
two-order-of-magnitude variation in the case counts themselves. The sizes of the
actual national cross-sections are not recorded in the results database, so the
8--4{,}000 range is chosen to bracket them rather than estimated from them.

Because $x_{\min}$ is measured in cases, it tracks the magnitude of the season,
and an exponent that co-varies with the magnitude of the season is to that extent
a re-encoding of it. This is the reason the ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} is the decisive test of the scaling block rather than
an optional addition to the descriptive result.

\subsection{The fitted law as a risk model}
\label{sec:riskmodel}

Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed} makes the descriptive result operational.
Figure~\ref{fig:exceed} confronts the exceedance curve implied by each national
fit with the empirical exceedance frequency of the same cross-section: the
fitted pair $(\alpha,x_{\min})$ reproduces the observed frequency of large
seasons over roughly two decades of epidemic size, and it does so with a
two-parameter summary of a cross-section of thousands of municipalities.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig09_exceedance}
\caption{The fitted power law as a probabilistic forecast of exceedance. For
each national cross-section, curves give

This is the sense in which the description is predictive before any machine
learning is involved: $\alpha$ is not a summary of central tendency but a
summary of how much of the risk sits in the tail, and it is available for every
municipality even when that municipality has never experienced an extreme
season.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The verdict belongs to the construction}

One estimator, one body of surveillance data, seven ways of cutting it, and
verdicts ranging from unanimous support for a power law (L6, 15 of 15 years) to
unanimous rejection in favour of an exponential (L7). Median exponents span
$1.95$ to $2.95$ across the levels, and the selected lower bounds run from a
single case to about $1.5\times 10^{5}$, five orders of magnitude. Nothing
distinguished the analyses except the construction: there was
no second data source, no rival estimator and no software difference to which
the disagreement could be attributed.

The sharpest form of the argument is the L1/L2 pair, which differ in nothing but
the length of the window applied to the same weekly counts. Pooled over a decade
the power law is preferred in 75\% of municipalities; cut into single seasons the
same counts prefer it in 23\%, while significant support for the exponential
rises from 4\% to 21\%. Between those two numbers not a single transmission
parameter changes. An analysis that reports the first without the second has
reported a property of its window.

We do not read this as showing that the published exponents are erroneous. The
individual estimates may each be correct arithmetic. What does not survive is the
inference from an exponent to a regime: the step that treats $\alpha$ as a
property of the pathogen or the place, when it is a property of the tuple
$(\text{unit},\ \text{window},\ \text{variable},\ \text{comparison model})$.

\subsection{Size and duration are different random variables}

The cross-sectional levels settle a second, quieter disagreement. Epidemic
\emph{size} and epidemic \emph{duration} are computed from the same municipal
episodes, and they give opposite verdicts under the same estimator. Size is
power-law with $\alpha\approx 2.0$ at national level and $1.97$ at state level.
Duration is not: the power law is rejected in all 15 national fits and in 95\% of
the 330 state-year fits, and the estimated exponent collapses onto a narrow band
($2.88$--$2.99$, SD $0.03$) that presses against the estimator's ceiling of $3$
in 11 of the 330 fits. That is what a bounded or exponential body returns when
forced through a power-law estimator. A near-constant fitted exponent
across independent cross-sections is not evidence of universality; it is evidence
that the model is being asked the wrong question.

Because both variables are routinely described as ``epidemic size'' or
``outbreak magnitude'' in the literature, part of the apparent contradiction
between published dengue exponents is plausibly the mixing of these two
distributions. The relevant distinction is not biological before it is
mathematical: a quantity bounded below by one week and above by the length of a
season cannot have the support that a scale-free law requires.

\subsection{What $\alpha$ is worth without its tuple}

The practical consequence is a reporting convention, and it is cheap to adopt.
An exponent should travel with the lower bound that produced it, the unit of
analysis, the number of observations, and the alternative model it was tested
against. Of these, $x_{\min}$ is the most informative and the most frequently
omitted, because it separates two analyses that look identical in a table of
results. At $x_{\min}=2$ the fitted power law describes counts of one and two
cases and the heavy-tail language is vacuous; at $x_{\min}=762$ it describes the
upper decade of a distribution and the language is warranted. The L1 and L3
constructions illustrate the point in a single line: their median exponents are
almost the same ($1.95$ and $2.09$, a difference of $0.14$) while their median
lower bounds differ by a factor of $170$ ($2$ versus $341$ cases). Two analyses
that would be reported as agreeing on the exponent are not describing the same
object.

The diagnostic is easy to apply and hard to game, since $x_{\min}$ is chosen by
the same goodness-of-fit criterion that selects the model and cannot be tuned to
produce a desired $\alpha$. We would go further: an $\alpha$ reported without
$x_{\min}$ should be treated as an incomplete statistic, in the same way that a
correlation is incomplete without its sample size.

\subsection{Why the coupling matters for prediction}

Two properties of the fitted pair must be separated, because the forecasting
argument depends on the first and is threatened by the second.

The first is that $\alpha$ is low-variance relative to its target. Across the 15
national cross-sections it ranges from 1.78 to 2.27, while annual case counts
over the same years vary by two orders of magnitude. On simulated samples of
800--4{,}000 points with the true exponent fixed at $2.0$, the sampling
dispersion of $\hat\alpha$ is about $0.03$ (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}); the
observed dispersion is roughly five times larger, so the year-to-year variation is real rather than noise, but it remains
small on the scale on which the target varies. That is the specific reason a
shape parameter can be a candidate predictor in a setting where the observables
are volatile.

The second is that $\alpha$ is not independent of $x_{\min}$. At the national
cross-section the rank correlation between the two is $0.87$ ($n=15$,
$p<0.001$). Within states it is $0.65$ at the median across the 24 states with at
least six years of data, positive in 88\% of them and significantly so in 67\%,
whereas between states it is only $0.22$. The coupling is therefore a within-unit
relationship over time, not a contrast between places.

This is not an artefact of the estimator. Repeating the fit on simulated data
drawn from a power law whose true exponent is held fixed at $2.0$, while the true
lower bound is varied across the same range, recovers no positive coupling
(Pearson $r=-0.20$, $n=40$) and an essentially unbiased exponent
(mean $2.012$). The correlation in the surveillance data is a property of the
data, not of the method.

The consequence for prediction is direct and uncomfortable. Because $x_{\min}$ is
measured in cases, it tracks the magnitude of the season: years in which
municipalities produce large epidemics place the cut higher. An exponent that
co-varies with the scale of the season is, to that extent, a re-encoding of the
scale of the season --- and the scale of the previous season is exactly
$S_{t-1}$, which is already present in the M2 rung. The descriptive case for
carrying $(\alpha,x_{\min})$ into a forecasting model is therefore weakened by
the same analysis that supports it: the block may be redundant with the lagged
counts it is meant to improve upon. This is a testable expectation, not an
objection, and it is the expectation with which the ablation ladder was designed.
If the scaling block earns its place, it must do so on the primary loss and
against a model that already knows how large last season was.

\subsection{What the descriptive result does license}

The exceedance curve, however, is not affected by that caveat, and it is the
part of the description that carries practical content. Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed}
turns $(\alpha,x_{\min})$ into a probability for every epidemic size, and the
15 national fits imply that between 5.4\% and 16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten
times $x_{\min}$ in a given year, and between 0.3\% and 2.7\% exceed a hundred
times it. Those are statements a surveillance service can act on and that a
subsequent season can falsify, and they require no fitted case count, only the
current cross-section.

It is worth being explicit about what follows, and what does not, from
$\alpha\approx2$. The familiar inference --- that a divergent second moment makes
extreme seasons unpredictable --- is too strong. It is a statement about second
moments and about squared-error loss, not about information, and an exponent
remains a scaling statement even where the variance diverges: branching-process
analysis shows that a power-law-tailed offspring distribution yields a
finite-time scaling law governed by the exponent alone \citep{falco2022finite}.
A power law with
$\alpha=2$ assigns a definite probability to every exceedance level; what it
denies is that the \emph{magnitude} of the largest event can be estimated from
the variance. Prediction of the tail event and prediction of its size are
different problems, and proper scoring rules keep them separate. The literature
that reads $\alpha\approx2$ as a verdict on forecastability conflates the two.

\subsection{Relation to existing forecast systems}

Operational dengue forecasting in Brazil has largely been built on climate and
population susceptibility, with machine-learning models predicting municipal
epidemics months ahead from weather and immunity proxies
\citep{mcgough2021dynamic}, and with nowcasting platforms supplying the adjusted
case series used here \citep{araujo2025large}. Those approaches ask what
covariates precede an outbreak. The question posed here is orthogonal: whether
the \emph{shape} of the size distribution carries information that the covariates
do not. The two are complementary rather than competing, and the ablation design
is deliberately constructed so that the scaling block is judged against a model
that already contains the climate and lagged-metric blocks.

There is also a methodological debt to the forecasting literature that the
power-law literature has not settled. Skill has to be measured with proper
scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007strictly} on paired loss differentials
\citep{diebold1995comparing}, not with the correlation of predicted and observed
point forecasts. With $\alpha\approx2$ the latter is dominated by the few largest
outbreaks and can be moved from excellent to poor by a single season.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations bound these conclusions.

\emph{The case series are nowcast-adjusted.} The variable analysed is the
estimated case count produced by the platform's nowcasting model, not the raw
notification count. This is the right series for a retrospective description,
because raw notifications are incomplete in recent weeks, but it means that the
upper tail inherits whatever smoothing the nowcast applies. If the nowcast
shrinks extreme values, the fitted exponents are biased towards the bulk, and the
size distribution reported here is somewhat less heavy than the notification
distribution would be.

\emph{The state level is an arithmetic aggregate.} State-level duration is the
mean of municipal durations and state peak week is the median of municipal peaks.
Neither is the duration or the peak of the aggregate epidemic, which is the
quantity a state health secretariat actually experiences. The state-level
results should be read as properties of the municipality distribution within a
state, not as properties of the state epidemic.

\emph{The cross-sections are short.} The national levels rest on 15 annual fits
and the state levels on three to 15 years per state, so the correlations reported
in Section~\ref{sec:coupling} are estimated on small samples even where the
underlying cross-sections contain thousands of municipalities. The
within-state estimates are more robust than the national ones because they pool
over 24 states.

\emph{The trees cannot extrapolate.} This limitation cuts across both halves of
the paper and deserves emphasis. Tree ensembles predict within the support of
their training responses. The season of greatest public-health interest is the
exceptional one, and under $\alpha\approx2$ the largest season a municipality
will experience is systematically outside the support of its observed history.
The models are therefore weakest precisely where the heavy tail places the risk,
and no amount of feature engineering removes that: it is a property of the
estimator class. It is also the reason the quantile models are reported alongside
the point forecasts, since a quantile regression at $\tau=0.95$ at least attempts
to represent the upper region rather than interpolating within the observed
range.

\emph{The classification threshold is a choice.} Exceeding the 75th percentile of
a unit's own history defines a large season; at the 90th percentile the positive
class becomes small enough that the balanced accuracy of any classifier falls,
and the comparison between rungs loses power. The threshold is therefore a
design parameter, fixed before the runs and reported with them.

\emph{The exponent cannot exceed three.} The fitting routine bounds $\alpha$
above by $3$, so a tail steeper than that is reported as $3$ rather than as its
unconstrained maximum. For the size results this is immaterial, since no
national size fit exceeds $2.27$; for durations it is not, and 11 of the 330
state-year fits sit at the bound. This does not weaken the conclusion that
durations are not power-law distributed --- a bound that the data press against
is itself evidence against the fitted family --- but the numerical value of those
particular exponents should not be read as an estimate.

\emph{Predictors are ecological and coarse.} No serotype composition, no
seroprevalence and no vector index enters the model, although all three are
plausible determinants of season size. To the extent that these are stable within
a unit, they are absorbed by unit-level effects; to the extent that they vary by
year, they are unmodelled heterogeneity that the scaling block may be
inadvertently proxying.

\subsection{Implications for surveillance practice}

Three practices follow, none of them expensive.

First, report the tuple. An exponent accompanied by its unit, its $x_{\min}$, its
sample size and its comparison model can be assessed by a reader; an exponent
alone cannot, and the seven levels here show that the same exponent can mean
opposite things.

Second, use $x_{\min}$ as a screening diagnostic before interpreting any heavy
tail, and treat $x_{\min}$ within the bulk of the data as a signal that the
question needs restating rather than a small technical detail.

Third, do not translate a heavy tail into a claim of unpredictability without
specifying the loss. A distribution without a finite variance constrains
squared-error point forecasts and leaves probabilistic exceedance statements
intact --- and the exceedance statement is usually the one a health service
needs, since it is the basis for deciding how many beds to hold in reserve.

\section{Conclusion}

The distribution of dengue epidemic size in Brazil is heavy-tailed with an
exponent close to two, and this is one of the few robust facts in the applied
literature on epidemic scaling. The distribution of epidemic duration is not
heavy-tailed at all. Which of these statements a study reports, and whether it
reports a power law or an exponential, is determined by the construction it
imposes on the data: seven levels applied to one dataset produced median
exponents between 1.95 and 2.95, unanimous support for a power law at one level
and unanimous rejection at another, with a single change of temporal window sufficient to move
the verdict from 75\% to 23\% support. That heterogeneity is methodological
before it is biological, and reporting $\alpha$ without its lower bound, its
unit, its sample size and its comparison model makes the disagreement
unresolvable.

Two consequences follow for forecasting. The encouraging one is that
$(\alpha,x_{\min})$ fixes the entire exceedance curve of a municipality, and the
national fits reproduce the observed frequency of extreme seasons over two
decades of epidemic size using two parameters. The cautionary one is that the
estimated exponent co-varies with the scale of the season within units, so a
model carrying $\alpha_{t-1}$ may be re-encoding $S_{t-1}$; whether it adds
information is an empirical question that the ablation ladder was built to
answer, and its answer is not presumed here.

What we can state is the form the answer must take. The claim that a scaling
description improves forecasts is only meaningful against a specified benchmark,
on a proper scoring rule, out of sample, with the scaling block isolated from the
blocks it is added to, and with every leakage channel closed --- including the
validation split, which fails silently. And the claim that a heavy tail makes
epidemics unpredictable should be retired in favour of the narrower one it
actually supports: that the size of the largest event is not estimable from the
second moment, while its probability is.
