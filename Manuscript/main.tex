\begin{abstract}
Studies of dengue report power-law distributions of epidemic size and read them as
evidence that large epidemics cannot be forecast. We separate the two statements and
test the second. For 5{,}571 Brazilian municipalities (2010--2026) we fit discrete
power laws to weekly case counts and to epidemic size under five constructions of
the series, selecting $x_{\min}$ by Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance and comparing each
fit against an exponential (50{,}391 fits).

The construction decides what the exponent describes. Median exponents range from
$1.95$ to $2.08$. Weekly counts follow a power law in 77\% of municipalities pooled
over a decade and in 23\% within single seasons, but in most of those fits the lower
bound falls at five cases or fewer, so the exponent describes the bulk and not a
tail. Epidemic size, the case total of one season, keeps a tail at every
construction tested: the power law is preferred in all 16 national seasons (median
exponent 2.03) and in 96\% of 353 state-years (1.98), with lower bounds two to three
orders of magnitude above the bulk.

Because the size exponent is close to two, the fitted pair $(\alpha_S, x_{\min})$
fixes the probability that a season exceeds any level $s$. It therefore defines a
risk curve; whether large epidemics can be forecast is a separate question, and the
exponent does not settle it. From the 16 national cross-sections the pair implies
that 5.6--16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times $x_{\min}$ in a given year and
0.3--2.7\% a hundred times it.

Forecasting depends on the target and on the spatial unit. For the municipal
question of whether a season will exceed the municipality's own upper quartile,
last season's descriptors, duration and peak timing among them, beat a baseline
of that municipality's own rate in four of five forecast years and lose in the
fifth, the only contrast here to satisfy the pre-specified test; the area under
the ROC curve rises from 0.50 to 0.58, and to 0.65 with every term. At state
level the same contrast satisfies the test in two of five years. For the
magnitude of the size no model reaches skill, and the power-law parameters add
nothing to one that already knows the previous season's size. They give the
probability of an extreme season and no prediction of next season's size.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The dynamics of infectious disease epidemics have generated an extensive
literature, both in mechanistic mathematical modelling and in statistical
inference \citep{siettos2013mathematical}. The former seeks to represent the
causal chain of transmission; the latter seeks to characterise the distribution
of observed incidence.

Among the most influential results of the mechanistic tradition is the basic
reproduction number $R_0$, which governs the speed of spread and defines the
epidemic threshold; in a vector-borne infection the threshold quantity rests on a
generation interval that varies with temperature, and must be estimated
accordingly \citep{codeco2018rt}. Because such models are seldom identifiable
from incidence alone, their parameters are usually obtained within a calibration
framework that returns a distribution rather than a point estimate
\citep{coelho2011bayesian}. In the statistical tradition, incidence is most often
modelled as a stochastic process, typically Poisson or negative binomial
\citep{becker1999statistical, li2012log}. The central difficulty in either case
is overdispersion: surveillance counts generate far more extreme observations
than tractable count models admit.

A recurrent claim is that this overdispersion is not merely large but
\emph{scale-free}, that incidence follows a power law
\citep{rhodes1997critical, clauset2009power, neipel2020power}. Where transmission
is itself power-law-tailed the exponent is not a descriptive curiosity: the
survival probability of an outbreak obeys a scaling law whose form is set by that
exponent \citep{falco2022finite}. The claim matters because a heavy tail implies
extremes that most models fail to predict, and because those extremes account for
most of the disease burden and most of its economic cost. The quantity that public
health planning acts on is the attack ratio, the fraction of the population
infected in a season, which has to be inferred from aggregated notification data
because serotype-specific immunity is unobserved \citep{coelho2015attack}. Were
the claim correct, it would bear directly on forecasting and on the allocation of
resources.

The empirical record is inconsistent. Reported exponents for dengue vary widely,
some studies find exponential rather than power-law behaviour, and the presence
of a heavy tail appears to depend on the study period, on the spatial unit and on
the outcome variable. This heterogeneity has generally been attributed to
biology or to geography, on the assumption that different places or different
years are governed by different regimes. In Brazil the geography has itself moved:
dengue has expanded towards higher latitudes and towards municipalities with no
prior transmission, so that consecutive seasons describe different sets of
municipalities~\citep{codeco2022expansion}.

We test a narrower set of claims, and we begin from a different premise: that a power law is not a statement about whether epidemics can be forecast but a
\emph{specification of risk}.
The exponent of epidemic size assigns a determinate probability to every exceedance level,
so that the fitted pair $(\alpha_S, x_{\min})$ summarises the entire risk curve of
a municipality's epidemic size using two parameters, in a setting where the
observables vary by two orders of magnitude between years. What a heavy tail precludes is that the
\emph{magnitude} of the largest event be estimated from the variance; what it
supplies is the probability that such an event occurs. These are distinct
problems, and a proper scoring rule keeps them distinct.

\subsection{Questions addressed}
\label{sec:questions}

We pose three questions in sequence, each conditional on the answer to the
preceding one.

First, \emph{what is the exponent an exponent of?} Any epidemiological series
admits several legitimate partitionings, the weekly counts of one
municipality, its annual epidemic totals, the set of epidemic sizes across
municipalities within one season, and these are different random variables
with different supports. We fit the same estimator to the same surveillance data
under five constructions, obtaining two exponents rather than one, $\alpha_W$ for
the weekly case count and $\alpha_S$ for the epidemic size (total cases in a season), and report each together
with the diagnostic ($x_{\min}$) that makes it interpretable (see section \ref{sec:estimation} for details on the estimation). Epidemic size, the quantity
surveillance services act on, is the most useful of the two. Other Epidemic metris, such as
duration and peak timing are not power-law distributed, as both are bounded by the
length of the season, and thus do not present a heavy tail.

Second, \emph{what do the power-law parameters provide?} If the pair $(\alpha, x_{\min})$ is used to define a risk curve, it should reproduce the empirical frequency of large seasons. We test that directly against the same cross-sections.

Third, \emph{does the tail improve forecasts?} We pose this question in the two forms in which it could plausibly succeed: as a classification of whether a municipality will exceed its own historical upper quartile, which is the risk statement itself; and as a quantile regression of epidemic size.
The evaluation tests each of the five seasons 2021--2025 against a model trained only on the seasons before it, using a four-rung ablation ladder and a decision rule fixed in advance.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{An epidemic size power-law exponent, and a criterion for usefulness} The same incidence series, grouped in five aggregation levels
(Table~\ref{tab:levels}), show that weekly case
counts are not well described by power-laws: the fitted lower bound collapses to five cases or
fewer in 77\% of \lvl{W-mun} and 81\% of \lvl{W-seas}, so the law describes the
bulk rather than a tail (Section~\ref{sec:verdicts}). Epidemic size behaves
differently: the power law is preferred to an exponential in 16 of 16 national
seasons and in 96\% of 353 state-seasons, with median exponents of $2.03$ and
$1.98$ and median lower bounds of 730 and 169 cases (Section~\ref{sec:size}). The
rule that separates the two cases is a reporting one: an exponent describes a
tail only when $x_{\min}$ is stated beside it (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic}).

\item \textbf{Two parameters that turn into a probability.} Equation
\eqref{eq:exceed} maps $(\alpha_S, x_{\min})$ onto $\Pr(S\ge s)$ for any level
$s$, which makes the fitted pair a risk curve rather than a description of the
data it came from, and the curve reproduces the observed frequency of extreme
seasons over two decades (Sections~\ref{sec:bridge} and~\ref{sec:riskmodel}). A
point forecast does not say how likely a season is to pass a stated threshold;
the curve does.

\item \textbf{Power-law parameters are not predictive} Within a
state, $\alpha_S$ and $\log_{10}x_{\min}$ are rank-correlated at
$0.64$ (median over 24 states), and at $0.89$ across national seasons, whereas
data simulated with $\alpha_S$ held fixed recover no such association
(Table~\ref{tab:coupling}). The pair is thus a stable summary of seasonal
magnitude, and $\alpha_S$ is not a signal independent of lagged incidence, which
is what a predictor in its own right would have to be
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}). This is the mechanism behind item~4.

\item \textbf{A size-class classifier that works at municipal scale.} Last season's
descriptors raise AUC from $0.50$ to $0.58$ ($0.65$ with every term) and beat the
historical-rate baseline in 4 of 5 forecast years, the only contrast in the study to
satisfy the pre-specified rule. At state scale the same contrast reaches 2 of 5 and
fails it (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\item \textbf{A risk curve, and how far it reaches.} The pair gives the probability
that a season exceeds any level $s$, and it describes the cross-section that
produced it rather than the next one (Section~\ref{sec:riskmodel}). Carried forward a
season without refitting it still returns the level of risk at $10x_{\min}$ (median
error 1.6~pp) and overstates the far tail at $100x_{\min}$ (1.0\% implied against
0.24\% observed). It is a risk statement, not a prediction of the next season's size.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data sources and study period}
\label{sec:data}

We analyse weekly dengue case counts assembled by the InfoDengue/Mosqlimate
platform \citep{araujo2025large} for the period from 1 January 2010 to 22 January
2026, for every Brazilian municipality represented in the platform. The analysed
variable is the nowcast-adjusted estimated number of cases, that is, the
platform's reconstruction of the number of infections occurring in each week
after correcting for reporting delay by a hierarchical model of the delay
distribution \citep{bastos2019reporting}; it is not the raw notification count. The
series were projected onto a common epidemiological week ending on Sunday. Weeks
without a reported case are retained in the series but excluded from
distributional fits, since the power-law family is defined on positive support.

Descriptors of each epidemic season were obtained from the platform's seasonal decomposition, which partitions a municipal series into epidemic episodes and reports, for each episode, its total number of cases, its duration in weeks and the week in which it peaked~\citep{araujo2025large}.
These descriptors are available for 16 years, 2011 to 2026, the last of them bounded by the data cut-off of 22 January 2026; the fit pooled over all years is reported separately and is excluded from every year count, so that each national cross-section corresponds to one calendar season.
Only epidemic size is fitted as a power law; duration and peak timing enter the analysis as predictors of size.

Municipal population figures are the estimates distributed with the platform (5{,}571 municipalities, one record with a zero entry excluded).
Population enters no power-law fit; it is used to compute the descriptive correlations of Section~\ref{sec:coupling} and, as $\log_{10}$ population, as one predictor in the forecasting models of Section~\ref{sec:targets}.

\subsection{Definitions and notation}
\label{sec:definitions}

Several quantities recur throughout the paper, and their meaning is fixed here. Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the notation.

\emph{Weekly count.} Let $c_{i,w}$ denote the estimated number of dengue cases
in municipality $i$ during epidemiological week $w$. It is the primitive
observation on which every construction in this paper is built.

\emph{Season.} A season, or epidemic year, $t$ is the set of epidemiological
weeks assigned to calendar year $t$; it contains at most 53 weeks. A season is
indexed by the calendar year of its weeks, not by the year in which an epidemic
peaks.

\emph{Epidemic size.} Let $S_{i,t}$ denote the total number of cases in the
epidemic episode of municipality $i$ in season $t$, as identified by the seasonal
decomposition. We refer to $S_{i,t}$ as the \emph{epidemic size}, and we use
\emph{annual total} as a synonym for it and for no other quantity. In particular,
$S_{i,t}$ is neither the weekly count $c_{i,w}$ nor the calendar-year sum of
$c_{i,w}$, which would also include cases notified outside any identified
episode. The distinction is operational as well as definitional: an episode is
identified only where the decomposition detects one, so municipality-seasons
without a detected episode contribute no observation to the size distribution,
whereas the weekly counts exist for every municipality and week.

\emph{Cross-section.} A cross-section at season $t$ is the set $\{S_{i,t}\}$ of
epidemic sizes of the municipalities within a stated spatial scope. The
\emph{national cross-section} contains every Brazilian municipality with an
identified episode in $t$; a \emph{state-year cross-section} contains the
municipalities of one state in one season. A cross-section is therefore a sample
of units at a fixed time, in contrast to a series, which is a sample of times at
a fixed unit.

\emph{Construction.} A construction is the triple (fitted variable, sampling
unit, dimension of pooling) that determines which random variable is estimated
and what each observation of the fitted sample represents.

\emph{The two exponents.} The estimator of Section~\ref{sec:estimation} is
applied to two different random variables, and the two exponents are kept apart
throughout. $\alpha_W$ is the exponent of the weekly count $c_{i,w}$, fitted at
constructions \lvl{W-mun} and \lvl{W-seas}; $\alpha_S$ is the exponent of the
epidemic size $S_{i,t}$, fitted at \lvl{S-mun}, \lvl{S-state} and \lvl{S-nat}.
The unsubscripted $\alpha$ names the exponent of the law
$p(x)\propto x^{-\alpha}$ under discussion, whatever the variable, and is used
where that variable is named in the same sentence or fixed by the equation.
Every statement in this paper about exceedance, risk or forecasting is a statement
about $\alpha_S$. $\alpha_W$ appears only where the construction, the diagnostic or
the reporting convention is at issue, and it never carries a risk statement here: at
its own constructions the lower bound collapses into the bulk, which is what
disqualifies it.

\emph{Lower bound and bulk.} The lower bound $x_{\min}$ is the threshold above
which the power-law form is assumed to hold, and the \emph{bulk} is the part of
the empirical distribution below it. A fit is \emph{tail-informative} when
$x_{\min}$ lies above the bulk, in the operational sense of
Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic}.

\emph{Duration and peak timing.} $D_{i,t}$ denotes the duration of the episode
in weeks and $P_{i,t}$ the epidemiological week of its maximum.

\subsection{Five aggregation levels}
\label{sec:levels}

The unit of analysis is the sample of positive observations passed to the estimator.
Table~\ref{tab:levels} defines the five aggregation levels used; each is a construction in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:definitions}, and the two terms are used interchangeably.
Two variables are fitted, both derived from the same municipal series: the weekly case count $c_{i,w}$ at constructions \lvl{W-mun} and \lvl{W-seas}, and the epidemic size $S_{i,t}$ at constructions \lvl{S-mun}, \lvl{S-state} and \lvl{S-nat}. Within each group the constructions differ only in the dimension along which the variable is pooled, which is what allows a contrast between two of them to be read as the effect of that dimension alone.

The paper therefore estimates two exponents, not one: $\alpha_W$ for the weekly case count and $\alpha_S$ for the epidemic size.
They are close in value --- the median across the five constructions spans $1.95$ to $2.08$, and the two groups overlap inside that span --- which is why the distinction has to be carried by the notation rather than by the number: the two are not estimates of one quantity, they are estimates of two quantities that happen to be nearly equal, and only $\alpha_S$ describes the risk of a large season (Section~\ref{sec:size}).

Every handle is read as a variable and a scope. The initial letter names the fitted variable, \emph{W} for the weekly count, \emph{S} for the epidemic size, and the remainder names the scope over which observations are pooled: \emph{mun} for a single municipality, \emph{seas} for a single season of a single municipality, \emph{state} for the municipalities of one state in one season, and \emph{nat} for the municipalities of the country in one season.
The handles name constructions and are not random variables: only $c_{i,w}$ and $S_{i,t}$ enter the likelihood.
They are used throughout for the five constructions, for the rows of Tables~\ref{tab:levels} and \ref{tab:main}, and for the panels of Figures~\ref{fig:verdicts}, \ref{fig:xmin} and \ref{fig:window}.

A construction is fixed by two independent decisions. The first selects the variable that enters the likelihood: the weekly count $c_{i,w}$ as observed, or the season total $S_{i,t}$, obtained by summing the weeks of the epidemic episode delimited by the seasonal decomposition described in Section~\ref{sec:data}.
The second selects the sample over which that variable is pooled: the weeks of one municipality, taken over the complete series or over a single season; the seasons of one municipality; or the municipalities of one state or of the whole country within a single season. The five constructions of Table~\ref{tab:levels} are the combinations in use, not the full crossing of the two decisions; a pairing omitted from the table, such as the weekly counts of a national cross-section, is one that no contrast in this paper requires.

Formally, let $\mathcal{M}$ be the set of municipalities and $\mathcal{M}_s\subseteq\mathcal{M}$ those of state $s$, $\mathcal{W}$ the epidemiological weeks of the study period and $\mathcal{W}_t\subset\mathcal{W}$ those of season $t$ (Section~\ref{sec:definitions}), and $\mathcal{E}_{i,t}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_t$ the weeks that the seasonal decomposition assigns to the epidemic episode of $i$ in $t$; write $\mathcal{T}_i=\{t:\mathcal{E}_{i,t}\neq\varnothing\}$ for the seasons in which $i$ has an identified episode. The size variable sums the weeks of the episode and
not those of the calendar year,
%
\[
S_{i,t}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{E}_{i,t}}c_{i,w},\qquad t\in\mathcal{T}_i,
\]
%
so that $S_{i,t}\le\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}_t}c_{i,w}$, with equality only when every notified case of the season falls inside the episode, and $S_{i,t}$ does not exist for $t\notin\mathcal{T}_i$. A municipality without an identified episode therefore contributes no value to any size construction, whereas its weeks still enter both weekly ones once the count is positive (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

The five samples those two decisions produce are
%
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{D}_{\text{W-mun}}(i)     &= \{c_{i,w} : w\in\mathcal{W},\ c_{i,w}\ge 1\},\\
\mathcal{D}_{\text{W-seas}}(i,t)  &= \{c_{i,w} : w\in\mathcal{W}_t,\ c_{i,w}\ge 1\};\\[2pt]
\mathcal{D}_{\text{S-mun}}(i)     &= \{S_{i,t} : t\in\mathcal{T}_i\},\\
\mathcal{D}_{\text{S-state}}(s,t) &= \{S_{i,t} : i\in\mathcal{M}_s,\ t\in\mathcal{T}_i\},\\
\mathcal{D}_{\text{S-nat}}(t)     &= \{S_{i,t} : i\in\mathcal{M},\ t\in\mathcal{T}_i\},
\end{align*}
%
where $i$ is a municipality, $s$ a state and $t$ a season; the first two fit the weekly count and the last three the epidemic size. Each sample is estimated on its own, so that every row of Table~\ref{tab:levels} carries its own $\alpha$ and $x_{\min}$, and no parameter is shared between constructions.

A sample enters the estimator only if it holds at least ten positive values, a floor applied identically at the five constructions; samples below it are discarded rather than fitted, which is one reason the fit counts of Table~\ref{tab:levels} differ from the number of municipalities the data hold.
The floor binds unevenly.
At \lvl{S-mun} it leaves out municipalities whose history is too short to define a distribution; at \lvl{S-state} it leaves out a state-season in which fewer than ten municipalities have an identified episode, so a state with fewer than ten municipalities contributes to no state-year fit, and the Federal District, a single municipality, appears in the pooled sample alone.

Two further samples pool across seasons as well, keeping the spatial scope fixed; they are stored under the sentinel year $0$ and excluded from every count of seasons:
%
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{D}_{\text{S-state}}(s,\cdot) &= \{S_{i,t}: i\in\mathcal{M}_s,\ t\in\mathcal{T}_i\},\\
\mathcal{D}_{\text{S-nat}}(\cdot)     &= \{S_{i,t}: i\in\mathcal{M},\ t\in\mathcal{T}_i\}.
\end{align*}
%
At \lvl{S-nat} every season clears the floor, so its pooled sample is the multiset union of the national cross-sections.
At \lvl{S-state} it is not the union of the per-season samples: the floor removes state-seasons from the fits of \lvl{S-state} and removes nothing from the pooled sample, which therefore holds observations that no state-year fit has seen.

Constructions \lvl{W-mun} and \lvl{W-seas} differ only in the temporal window over which the weekly case counts are pooled: \lvl{W-mun} uses the complete available series of a municipality (January 2010 to January 2026), whereas \lvl{W-seas} restricts the sample to a single season, of at most 53 weeks. Their sample sizes differ by roughly an order of magnitude, which is why the comparison between them is made on shares rather than on counts.

Constructions \lvl{S-mun}, \lvl{S-state} and \lvl{S-nat} fit the same variable, $S_{i,t}$, and differ only in the dimension of pooling. \lvl{S-mun} pools the seasons of one municipality and is therefore a statement about how a single municipality's epidemic sizes vary over time; a municipality enters this construction only if at least ten seasons are available.
\lvl{S-state} and \lvl{S-nat} pool municipalities within a period, at state and national scope respectively, and are therefore statements about how sizes vary across space in a given season.
This nesting is deliberate: each contrast reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} is a single step within one of the two groups, so that a difference in the inferred regime is attributable to the construction rather than to a change of data source, period or variable.

\subsection{Discrete power-law estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

For each sample we fit a discrete power law by maximum likelihood following
\citet{clauset2009power}. The fitted probability mass function for integer
$x \ge x_{\min}$ is
%
\begin{equation}
p(x) = \frac{x^{-\alpha}}{\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})},
\label{eq:pl}
\end{equation}
%
where $\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})$ is the generalised (Hurwitz) zeta function, which
normalises the discrete mass. The exponent $\alpha$ is estimated by numerical
maximisation of the likelihood, and the lower bound $x_{\min}$ is selected
jointly by minimising the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the empirical and
fitted cumulative distributions over all candidate values of $x_{\min}$. Because
the observations are counts, the discrete formulation is used throughout; the
continuous approximation is not employed at any construction.

One property of the estimator must be stated for the table of results to be read
correctly. The numerical optimisation constrains $\alpha$ to the interval
$(1, 3]$: an estimate cannot exceed $3$, and a sample whose likelihood is
maximised beyond that value returns $\alpha=3$ rather than its unconstrained
maximum. The bound is immaterial for epidemic size, the variable on which the
substantive conclusions rest. At the size constructions no fit approaches it, the
largest values being $2.94$ at state level and $2.25$ at national level; at the
two weekly constructions it is reached by 12 of the 48{,}937 fits, or
$0.02\%$. The exponents reported in this paper are accordingly unconstrained
maxima and not boundary values.

The choice of $x_{\min}$ is not a nuisance parameter. It determines which
fraction of the data the model actually describes, and therefore whether the
fitted exponent refers to a tail at all. We therefore retain $x_{\min}$ for every
fit and report its median and the proportion of fits with $x_{\min} \le 5$ at
each construction.

\subsection{Comparison against an alternative distribution}
\label{sec:comparison}

A power law can always be fitted; the question is whether it describes the data
better than a plausible alternative. We compare each power-law fit against an
exponential distribution, which is the natural null for epidemiological counts
because it corresponds to a constant hazard of termination and generates a light
tail. The comparison follows the normalised likelihood-ratio procedure of
\citet{clauset2009power},
%
\begin{equation}
R = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\ln p_{\mathrm{PL}}(x_i) - \ln p_{\mathrm{exp}}(x_i)\right],
\label{eq:lr}
\end{equation}
%
normalised so that its magnitude is comparable across samples of different size.
The sign of $R$ indicates the preferred model, with $R>0$ favouring the power law
and $R<0$ the exponential, and its associated $p$-value quantifies whether the
sign is distinguishable from sampling noise.

We adopt a deliberately conservative reporting convention. A construction is
described as \emph{power-law supported} only when $R>0$ and $p<0.05$, as
\emph{exponential supported} only when $R<0$ and $p<0.05$, and as
\emph{inconclusive} otherwise. This prevents the common error of counting a
positive but non-significant $R$ as evidence for scaling.

\subsection{Identification diagnostic}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

A fit is flagged as \emph{tail-informative} when $x_{\min}$ exceeds the bulk of
the observed distribution, operationalised as $x_{\min}>5$ cases for weekly
counts and as $x_{\min}$ above the median observed value for the size
constructions. Where $x_{\min} \le 5$, the fitted power law is being asked to
describe the entire empirical distribution, including the small-count regime in
which the discrete power law is not a plausible generative model, since counts of
one or two cases arise from reporting mechanics rather than from scale-free
branching. Such fits are retained in the analysis but excluded from substantive
interpretation, and the proportion concerned is reported explicitly.

\subsection{From description to prediction: the exceedance curve}
\label{sec:bridge}

A fitted power law is not merely a description of a histogram; it is a statement
about exceedance probabilities. For integer $s \ge x_{\min}$,
%
\begin{equation}
\Pr(S \ge s) \;=\; \frac{\zeta(\alpha,s)}{\zeta(\alpha,x_{\min})},
\label{eq:exceed}
\end{equation}
%
so that a single pair $(\alpha, x_{\min})$ parameterises the probability that a
municipality experiences an epidemic of at least any given size. Four
consequences of Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed} shape the design of the forecasting
evaluation.

First, the size exponent summarises \emph{risk} rather than \emph{activity}. Two
municipalities with the same expected annual incidence but different $\alpha_S$
face different probabilities of a catastrophic season, and that difference is
invisible to the mean. The fitted models quantify the claim rather than merely
asserting it: applied to the 16 national size fits, Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed}
implies that between 5.6\% and 16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times
$x_{\min}$ in a given year, and that between 0.3\% and 2.7\% exceed one hundred
times $x_{\min}$. These are falsifiable implications of the description, and
Figure~\ref{fig:exceed} confronts them with the empirical exceedance frequencies.

Second, $\alpha_S$ is a low-variance quantity \emph{relative to its target}.
Across the national cross-sections it takes values between $1.78$ and $2.25$, over
a period in which annual case counts vary by two orders of magnitude. The weekly
exponent is in the same position across municipalities: $\alpha_W$ is essentially unrelated
to municipality size ($r=-0.043$ over $5{,}264$ municipalities,
Section~\ref{sec:coupling}), so an exponent is not a restatement of how large a
municipality is, and $\alpha_S$ is not a restatement of how heavily burdened it
is. The dispersion of $\hat\alpha_S$ is not zero --- it exceeds sampling noise by
roughly a factor of five in standard deviation, and it co-varies with the selected
$x_{\min}$ (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}) --- but it is small along precisely the
dimension in which the target is volatile. That is the specific reason to carry
$\alpha_S$ into the predictive models rather than treating it as a descriptive
curiosity, and the same coupling is the reason the ablation, rather than the
correlation, has to decide the question.

Third, $\alpha_S\approx2$ has a direct methodological consequence for evaluation.
The second moment of a power law with $\alpha \le 2$ does not exist in the
continuous limit, and at $\alpha \approx 2$ the sample mean is unstable, so that
squared-error losses are decided by the few largest outbreaks. A model that is
adequate everywhere except in the tail can therefore appear excellent or
inadequate depending on which years fall in the test set. We consequently
evaluate on quantile (pinball) losses and on the classification of tail events,
and treat the root mean squared error as a secondary, tail-dominated diagnostic.

Finally, the predictive hypothesis is stated in a form that can fail. If
$(\alpha_S, x_{\min})$ carries information about the coming season beyond what
lagged counts already encode, a model containing them should outperform an
otherwise identical model without them, out of sample. If instead $\alpha_S$ is
close to a deterministic function of the lower moments of the same series, the
two models should be indistinguishable, and the descriptive result would carry no
forecasting content. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} specifies the comparison that
decides between these outcomes, and Section~\ref{sec:leakage} lists the four ways
in which such a comparison could be falsified and how each is prevented.

\subsection{Predictive target and predictors}
\label{sec:targets}

The forecasting problem is posed at the level of an administrative unit, a
municipality or a state, and a target season $t$: every predictor is measured in
season $t-1$ or earlier, and the target is a property of the season of year $t$.
Where a statement refers to one of the two levels it names that level, states at
state level and municipalities at municipal level, and ``unit'' is reserved for the
places where the statement holds at either.
There is one target, the epidemic size $S_t$ defined in
Section~\ref{sec:definitions}, because it is the quantity surveillance services
act on and the only one of the three seasonal descriptors with unbounded support.
Epidemic duration $D_t$ and peak timing $P_t$ are predictors, not outcomes: they
describe the phenology of the previous season, and the ladder asks whether that
phenology helps forecast the magnitude of the next one.

Predictors fall into six blocks, listed in Table~\ref{tab:features}: lagged
seasonal descriptors of the previous season, which include its duration and peak
timing; the scaling descriptors $(\alpha_{S,t-1}, x_{\min,S,t-1})$ obtained from
the power-law fit to the previous season; the historical trend
$\dot\alpha_{t-1}$, defined as the ordinary least-squares slope of $\alpha_S$ over
all seasons strictly before $t$ and set to zero where fewer than two such seasons
exist; the epidemiological driver $R_{0,t-1}$ reported by the seasonal
decomposition; $\log_{10}$ population; and eight quarterly climate aggregates
from year $t-1$, namely mean minimum temperature and mean minimum humidity in each
of the four quarters. Units lacking the mandatory lags, that is without a previous
season, are excluded rather than imputed; the optional $\dot\alpha$ is set to zero
where it is undefined.

The ladder of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} groups these six blocks into three
cumulative rungs. M1 carries the lagged descriptors, the epidemiological driver and
population; M2 adds the climate block; M3 adds the two scaling blocks. Duration and
peak timing therefore enter at the first rung, as part of the lagged descriptors,
and each contrast between consecutive rungs is attributable to the block that the
later rung adds.

\subsection{Regression estimator}
\label{sec:regression}

The choice of estimator is a design decision in this problem and not a tuning
detail, because the quantity of interest can lie outside the range of the data to
which the model is fitted. Under $\alpha \approx 2$ the season of greatest
public-health interest is systematically larger than any season in the training
record, so the forecast must be allowed to take a value beyond the largest
response observed. The estimator is therefore a quantile regression on a
cubic spline basis with linear boundary extrapolation
\citep{koenker1978regression}. It minimises the pinball loss. Outside the
observed range the boundary segment continues linearly, which on the
$\log(1+S)$ scale is a multiplicative extrapolation on the scale of cases. The two
predictors expressed in cases, $S_{t-1}$ and $x_{\min,t-1}$, are logged before
entering the basis, without which the extrapolation would be linear in the wrong
scale. The $L_1$ penalty is $10^{-4}$, deliberately weak.

Quantile models are fitted on the grid
$\tau \in \{0.05, 0.10, \dots, 0.95\}$, which yields a full predictive
distribution per municipality and season rather than a point forecast, and all stochastic
components were seeded identically across runs. Figure~\ref{fig:predobs} shows
the resulting out-of-sample forecasts for the most recent season, in aggregate
and broken down by state, over the municipal panel. The boundary
segment is what allows a forecast to be placed above the largest annual total in
the training window, the region where the seasons of greatest concern lie.

\subsection{Classification of large seasons}
\label{sec:classifier}

The second predictive task is posed as a decision problem rather than as an
estimation problem: will unit $i$ experience, in season $t$, a season larger than
the $p$-th percentile of its own historical distribution? The target is
%
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,t} \;=\; \mathbb{I}\!\left[\,S_{i,t} > Q_p\big(\{S_{i,k}\}_{k<t}\big)\,\right],
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
%
with $p=0.75$ throughout. The threshold is a within-fold quantity: it is
recomputed from the training seasons of each fold and of each split, never from
the full sample, so that the label of a test observation cannot depend on
information unavailable to the model. The same discipline governs the
class-imbalance weight, set to the ratio of negative to positive observations in
the training fold.

The scope over which the percentile is taken is set by what each level can support.
At municipal level $Q_p$ is computed from each municipality's own training seasons, and
3{,}976 of the 5{,}571 municipalities supply the five seasons that estimate requires.
At state level the panel supplies 23 to 25 states per season, too few for a within-state
percentile, and $Q_p$ is taken across the states of the fold's training seasons. The
two labels therefore answer slightly different questions, and the state-level task is
the closer of the two to a ranking by state size (Section~\ref{sec:classresults}).

The classifier is a penalised logistic regression on the same spline basis, with
the class imbalance handled by weighting the training rows by the
negative-to-positive ratio. As with the regressor, the specification must remain
informative beyond the range covered by the training rows, and the logistic form
does so by extrapolating linearly in log-odds, so that a season far outside the
range of the panel is not forced towards the base rate observed in training.
Validation is an expanding-window split defined on the season index: fold $j$
trains on every unit-season earlier than $v_j$ and validates on season $v_j$
alone, where the $v_j$ are the $k=\min\{5,\, n_{\text{seasons}}-2\}$ most recent
training seasons. A fold
therefore exists only once at least two earlier seasons are available, and the
number of folds is capped at five. Because the folds are a function of the season
index rather than of arbitrary ordering, they are invariant to the ordering of the
observations.

This distinction is not cosmetic, and it is the step of the design whose failure
is silent. A split defined by arbitrary ordering rather than by the season index
produces, in a frame ordered by unit, a grouping by municipality: each fold then
trains on some municipalities across the whole calendar and validates on others
over the same calendar, so that validation contains no season absent from
training. The quantity estimated becomes transportability to new municipalities
rather than temporal generalisation, and the estimate depends on an undeclared
ordering of the rows. The defect is narrower but not absent when the frame
happens to be ordered by season, since contiguous blocks then still cut through
calendar years. The split is therefore indexed explicitly by the season, and any
validation season that is not strictly later than the training seasons of its
fold is treated as an error rather than as a warning. The decision threshold
applied to the predicted probability is chosen by Youden's $J$ statistic
\citep{youden1950index}, computed on the out-of-fold predictions rather than on
the training fit, and then held fixed for the test set. We report the area under
the ROC curve, the area under the precision--recall curve, which is the more
informative summary under class imbalance, the Brier score
\citep{gneiting2007strictly}, defined in Section~\ref{sec:brier},
the $F_1$ score at the Youden threshold and a
calibration curve, and we test differences in the area under the curve between
nested predictor sets by the method of \citet{delong1988comparing}.

\paragraph{The Brier score: definition, reference value and three uses.}
\label{sec:brier}

Let $Y_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ be the label of Equation~\eqref{eq:label} for unit-season
$i$, and let $p_{i}^{(M)}\in[0,1]$ be the probability that rung $M$ assigns to
$Y_{i}=1$. At season $y$, over the $n_{y}$ evaluated unit-seasons, the
Brier score of rung $M$ is the mean squared error of those probabilities,
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BS}(M,y)\;=\;\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}
\bigl(p_{i}^{(M)}-Y_{i}\bigr)^{2}.
\label{eq:brier}
\end{equation}
%
The score lies in $[0,1]$ and lower is better. The lower endpoint is reached only
by a forecast that is certain and right on every case, and the upper only by one
that is certain and wrong on every case. The score is a strictly proper scoring
rule \citep{gneiting2007strictly}: its expectation is minimised when $p_{i}$
equals the true conditional probability $\Pr(Y_{i}=1)$, so no rung lowers its
expected score by reporting a probability other than the one it estimates.
Properness is what allows the probability target of Section~\ref{sec:bridge} and
the size target of Section~\ref{sec:targets} to be compared by the same
statistic, Equation~\eqref{eq:skill}, since both numerators are proper losses.

The scale of the score is set by the forecast that carries no covariate. A
constant probability $p$, in a season where the event has frequency $q_{y}$,
scores $q_{y}(1-p)^{2}+(1-q_{y})p^{2}$. That expression is minimised at
$p=q_{y}$, where it equals $q_{y}(1-q_{y})$, the score of a forecast that knows
the season's frequency and nothing else. Rung M0 reports the training base rate
$\hat q_{y}$, which is the same expression evaluated at $p=\hat q_{y}$. In 2024
the two values differ sharply: $q_{y}=0.695$ against $\hat q_{y}=0.264$, so M0
scores $0.40$ where the frequency-only forecast would score $0.21$
(Section~\ref{sec:classresults}).

Three uses of the score recur in this paper, and all three are oriented so that a
positive value means the information under test helps. For a contrast between two
rungs, $\Delta\mathrm{BS}(M',M,y)=\mathrm{BS}(M,y)-\mathrm{BS}(M',y)$ is positive
when the later rung $M'$ has the lower score; this is the differential of
Table~\ref{tab:exceed}, whose municipal M1--M0 contrast has median $+0.049$ and
is positive, with an interval excluding zero, in 4 of the 5 seasons. Permutation
importance reports $\mathrm{BS}(M,y;\text{permuted})-\mathrm{BS}(M,y)$, the
increase in the score when a predictor or a block is shuffled in the held-out
season, and is positive when the model uses what is destroyed
(Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). Equation~\eqref{eq:skill} takes the score as its
numerator. Skill is reported on this loss rather than on the area under the curve
because an area is invariant to monotone transformations of the predicted
probabilities: a change in calibration that leaves the ordering of units intact
moves the score and not the area.

Feature attribution uses the same held-out permutation importance, reported both
per predictor and for the scaling block permuted as a unit
\citep{breiman2001random}: under the collinearity documented in
Section~\ref{sec:coupling}, a per-column ranking alone understates the importance
of a block whose members can substitute for one another.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and ablation ladder}
\label{sec:ablation}

A single temporal split cannot distinguish a predictor that helps from a
predictor that happened to help once. We therefore evaluate season by season:
for each season $y \in \{2021, \dots, 2025\}$ the model is trained on every
season earlier than $y$ and evaluated on season $y$, and the per-season losses
are pooled. This provides five out-of-sample seasons and allows
skill to be read as a function of the history available to the model.

Within each season, the incremental value of the scaling description is isolated
by the ablation ladder of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. Each rung adds one block of
predictors to the preceding one, so that a contrast between consecutive rungs is
attributable to the block that was added. The rung of substantive interest is
M3 against M2, which differ \emph{only} in the presence of
$(\alpha_{S,t-1}, x_{\min,S,t-1}, \dot\alpha_{t-1})$.

Forecast values at M0 are scaled by the empirical $\tau$-quantile of the
within-municipality season-on-season ratio, estimated on the training seasons, so that the
same benchmark can be evaluated at every quantile level rather than only at the
median. At $\tau=0.5$ the scale factor is close to unity and M0 reduces to plain
persistence; at the other levels this is the honest analogue of persistence and
not a deliberately weak comparator.

M0 is deliberately naive, because a benchmark that is already sophisticated
manufactures skill for whatever is added to it. Skill is reported as
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SS} \;=\; 1-\frac{L(\text{model})}{L(\mathrm{M0})},
\label{eq:skill}
\end{equation}
%
on the primary loss of each task: the pinball loss at $\tau=0.5$ for the
regression target and the Brier score for the classifier, both of which are proper
scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007strictly}. Secondary losses are the mean absolute
error and the root mean squared error of the point forecasts, the mean absolute
scaled error of \citet{hyndman2006another} to make errors comparable across units
of very different size, and the pinball loss at each $\tau$ of the grid.

Because units differ by orders of magnitude in scale, all model-versus-model
comparisons are made on paired loss differentials computed within the same unit
and season; their uncertainty is estimated by a bootstrap that resamples
\emph{units} rather than observations, preserving the within-unit correlation
across seasons, with 2{,}000 replicates. For the comparison pooled across seasons
we additionally report the Diebold--Mariano statistic \citep{diebold1995comparing}
with a Newey--West correction, since the loss series are serially correlated by
construction.

The decision rule is fixed in advance. The scaling block is judged to add
predictive value only if M3 improves on M2 on the primary loss with a 95\%
bootstrap interval for the paired difference that excludes zero, in the same
direction, for at least 3 of the 5 evaluation seasons. This is stricter than
a single significant pooled test, and deliberately so: a pooled test can be
carried by one anomalous season, which is precisely the failure mode that a
heavy-tailed target invites.

\subsection{Leakage controls}
\label{sec:leakage}

Four sources of leakage are structurally possible in this design, and each is
closed explicitly.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Contemporaneous scaling descriptors.} $\alpha_{S,t}$ is estimated
from the weekly counts of season $t$, which are part of the target. Only
$\alpha_{S,t-1}$ enters as a predictor, and $\dot\alpha_{t-1}$ is computed
exclusively from seasons strictly before $t$.
\item \textbf{The classification threshold.} $Q_p$ is computed inside each fold
from training seasons only. Computing it once on the full sample would leak the
distribution of the test seasons into the labels of the training seasons,
inflating apparent skill by construction.
\item \textbf{Cross-sectional information.} The cross-section of municipalities
within season $t$ is an outcome of that season and is therefore never a
predictor; the scaling descriptors always come from the previous season's
cross-section.
\item \textbf{The validation split itself.} Folds are defined on the season index
(Section~\ref{sec:classifier}), so that no fold trains on a season at or after the
season it validates on. This is the only one of the four channels that fails
silently in a plausible implementation --- every number still resembles a
respectable validation score --- and it is therefore enforced rather than merely
documented.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Epidemic size follows a power law with an exponent near two}

significant, with median $\alpha_S=2.03$ (IQR 1.90--2.10) and
median $x_{\min}=730$ cases. $\alpha_S$ is stable, ranging from 1.78 to 2.25 over
sixteen seasons in a period in which annual case counts vary by two orders of
magnitude. At state level the picture is consistent and weaker in
significance, as expected from smaller cross-sections: the power law is preferred
in 96\% of the 353 state-year cross-sections and significant in 52\%, with median
$\alpha_S=1.98$ and median $x_{\min}=169$ cases.

This is the object with which the remainder of the paper works, and the reason is
worth stating precisely. The median lower bound of 730 cases at national level
and 169 at state level places the fit well above the bulk of a distribution whose
observations span four orders of magnitude: what is described is the upper region
in which the risk is located, not the body of small seasons. This is also the
sense in which the two groups of constructions are not interchangeable. At
$x_{\min}=2$ the fitted law describes counts of one and two cases; at
$x_{\min}=730$ it describes the upper decade of a distribution. Two analyses
reported as agreeing on the exponent can therefore be describing entirely
different objects: \lvl{W-mun} and \lvl{S-mun} have median exponents, $\alpha_W$ and
$\alpha_S$ respectively, of $1.95$ and $2.08$, a difference of $0.13$, while their
median lower bounds differ by a factor of $150$. The empirical complementary
cumulative distribution of Figure~\ref{fig:ccdf} shows the same fact graphically, as
a straight line over two decades above $x_{\min}$.

\subsection{What the size exponent represents: a readout of seasonal scale}
\label{sec:coupling}

The fitted pair is not a pair of independent coefficients, and the direction of
their dependence determines how the description can be used.
Table~\ref{tab:coupling} collects the association between the two estimates at the
levels where it can be measured, together with the association recovered from
simulated data in which the true $\alpha_S$ was held fixed.

The association is strong at national level and strong \emph{within} states over
time --- positive in 92\% of the 24 states with at least six seasons of data, and
significantly positive in 67\% --- but weak between states. It is therefore a
within-municipality, over-time relationship and not a contrast between places. The
control recovers no such association, so the estimator does not generate it: it
places the sampling dispersion of $\hat\alpha_S$ at $0.03$ (40 replicates, samples
of 800 to 4{,}000 points, true $\alpha_S$ fixed at $2.0$), against an observed
standard deviation of $0.150$ across the 16 national fits. The season-to-season
movement in $\alpha_S$ is real, roughly five times the sampling noise in standard
deviation, even though it remains small next to the variation in the case counts
themselves.

Read as description, this is the most informative single result about
$(\alpha_S, x_{\min})$ in the paper. Because $x_{\min}$ is measured in cases, it
tracks the magnitude of the season: seasons in which municipalities produce large
epidemics place the threshold higher, and $\alpha_S$ moves with it. The pair is
therefore a two-number summary of how large a season was, available for every
municipality, far less volatile than the case count it summarises, and stable
enough to be compared across seasons. Read as a
predictor set, the same fact is a warning: an $\alpha_S$ that co-varies with the
scale of the season is, to that extent, a re-encoding of $S_{t-1}$, which any
model of size already carries. Both readings are correct, and they make different
predictions about the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:prediction}.

The weekly exponent $\alpha_W$ is essentially unrelated to municipality size
(Pearson $r=-0.043$, Spearman $-0.048$ against $\log_{10}$ population; $n=5{,}264$)
and only weakly related to incidence
(Pearson $-0.143$, Spearman $-0.248$; $n=4{,}663$). Larger or more heavily burdened
municipalities are therefore not in a different scaling regime, and $\alpha_W$ is
not a proxy for how much transmission a place sees. It is a property of the
construction; $\alpha_S$ is, within a unit, a property of the scale of the season.

\subsection{The fitted law as a risk curve, and how far it travels}
\label{sec:riskmodel}

Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed} makes the description operational. A power law with
exponent $\alpha_S$ and lower bound $x_{\min}$ assigns
$\Pr(S \ge s)=\zeta(\alpha_S,s)/\zeta(\alpha_S,x_{\min})$ for every level $s$, so that
the fitted pair is a complete statement about the frequency of extreme seasons. It
requires no fitted case count, no covariate and no training period: it is
available from the current cross-section alone.

Figure~\ref{fig:exceed} confronts the exceedance curve implied by each national
fit with the empirical exceedance frequency of the same cross-section. The agreement
holds over roughly two decades of epidemic size. Part of it is by construction:
$x_{\min}$ was chosen to minimise the distance between the two curves, so the figure
measures the size of the agreement rather than testing the choice of family. That a
curve with two parameters reaches this far is the result.
Numerically, the 16 national fits imply that between 5.6\% and 16.5\% of municipalities
exceed ten times $x_{\min}$ in a given season, and that between 0.3\% and 2.7\% exceed
one hundred times it. These are statements on which a surveillance service can act
and which a subsequent season can falsify.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig09_exceedance}
\caption{The fitted power law as a probabilistic statement about exceedance. Epidemic size
$S$ is the annual total of a municipality and $s$ a size threshold; the curves
give, for three national cross-sections (2016, 2019 and 2024),
$\Pr(S\ge s)=\zeta(\alpha_S,s)/\zeta(\alpha_S,x_{\min})$ evaluated at the fitted

\label{fig:exceed}

This is the sense in which the description is predictive before any statistical
learning is involved: $\alpha$ is not a summary of central tendency but a summary
of how much of the risk lies in the tail, and it is available for every
municipality even where that municipality has never experienced an extreme
season. The familiar inference, that a divergent second moment renders extreme
seasons unpredictable, is a statement about squared-error loss and not about
information. Prediction of a tail event and prediction of its magnitude are
distinct problems.

The reach of the curve is fixed by the season that produced it. Carried forward one
season without refitting, over the 14 consecutive pairs that hold enough
municipalities above the fitted bound to be evaluated, it returns the level of risk
at ten times the bound with a median absolute error of 1.6 percentage points, and it
overstates the far tail: at a hundred times the bound it implies a median share of
1.0\% where the following season delivers 0.24\%. A pair describes the cross-section
in which it was estimated, and that is the cross-section a service holds when it
plans the coming season.

\subsection{Predicting the probability of a large season}
\label{sec:classresults}

The exceedance formulation has a direct predictive counterpart: rather than
asking how large the season will be, ask whether it will fall in the upper
quartile of the unit's own history. This is the risk statement itself, and it is
the natural place to look for value in the scaling descriptors, since a tail
exponent is by construction a statement about upper-tail probabilities.

We posed the question at the two levels where it is asked operationally. At state
level (Table~\ref{tab:exceed}) the lagged seasonal descriptors, which include
the previous season's duration and peak timing as ordinary covariates, alongside
size, $R_0$ and population, move the Brier score in the expected direction: a
median of $+0.126$, positive in 4 of 5 seasons and reaching $+0.212$. The
intervals exclude zero in only 2 of the 5 seasons, against the 3 that the rule requires, so the rung fails the rule at state level: each season is a
bootstrap over 23 to 25 states and the median interval is $0.26$ wide, wider than
the effect it is asked to resolve. Climate adds nothing to it (median $+0.027$) and neither does the scaling block
(median of $+0.000$, with no season in which the block improves by an interval excluding
zero). The per-season
differentials for M1 against M0 are $-0.064$, $+0.074$, $+0.126$, $+0.212$ and $+0.149$ for 2021 to 2025, negative in the first season and positive thereafter,
which is the pattern expected of a model that requires history in order to be
trained.

At municipal level the same ladder runs over 3{,}976 municipalities with enough
history for a within-municipality threshold, at least five seasons, with between
580 and 1{,}484 municipalities evaluated per season. Two things change, and only
one of them concerns the scaling descriptors. The first is a correction. With
the threshold taken from each municipality's own seasons, the population-only
reference falls to a median area under the curve of $0.47$ (range
$0.454$--$0.522$), against $0.87$ (range $0.84$--$0.93$) where the threshold is
pooled across municipalities, as it must be at state level: a pooled label is
largely a restatement of municipality size, whereas the within-municipality
label is not. The second is that the lagged block, which the state level could
not resolve, satisfies the rule here. Its Brier differential has a median of
$+0.049$, positive and with an interval excluding zero in 4 of 5 seasons, the
only contrast in this study to satisfy the pre-specified rule
(Table~\ref{tab:exceed}). Discrimination rises from $0.50$ for the no-skill
reference to $0.58$, $0.63$ and $0.65$ for M1, M2 and M3; M3 is the best of the
three in 3 of 5 seasons and gains a median $+0.013$ over M2.

The inversion between the two levels is a statement about precision and not about
epidemiology. The state estimate of the same effect is the larger of the two in
absolute terms, $+0.126$ against $+0.049$, and the less certain by a factor of
eight in interval width, $0.26$ against $0.034$, so that a rule which fails at one
level and passes at the other is failing on sample size: twenty-five states per
season cannot resolve an effect that 1{,}484 municipalities can. The two levels also differ in
the scope of the label, which is pooled across states but taken within each
municipality, so the state comparison inherits the size ranking described above and is
the weaker of the two tests of epidemic rarity. What the two levels agree on is the
ranking that matters for this paper. The scaling block satisfies the criterion at
neither level: at municipal level it moves in the expected direction in 5 of 5 seasons,
by a median of $+0.002$, but its interval excludes zero in only one of them, and at
state level it moves in the expected direction in 1 of 5 and has an interval excluding
zero in 2, both against the block. Whether a municipality will experience an
exceptional season is in part predictable from how large the previous one was; the
exponent does not add to that.

One season requires comment. In 2024, 69.5\% of the evaluated
municipalities exceeded their own upper quartile, against a training base rate of
26.4\%. The no-skill reference scores a Brier score of $0.40$ there and the fitted rungs about $0.25$, better than the reference but worse than the $0.21$ that a
predictor knowing the season's own frequency would earn. That season was a
national record, and no model trained on earlier seasons anticipated its level.
This is the boundary of the exercise: the classifier ranks municipalities within a season
of familiar magnitude; it does not anticipate a change of regime.

Figures~\ref{fig:roc} and~\ref{fig:importance} report discrimination, calibration
and attribution for the municipal classifier. The calibration panel is the
relevant one: a classifier useful for planning must track the diagonal in the
upper deciles, not merely rank well on average.

Attribution needs its own reading, and Figure~\ref{fig:importance} is easy to
misread as a list of failures. 11 of the 16 individual predictors have a negative
median permutation: permuting them \emph{lowers} the Brier score. The three most
detrimental are $x_{\min,t-1}$, the peak week $P_{t-1}$ and the previous total
$S_{t-1}$, with medians of $-0.087$, $-0.047$ and $-0.036$, all three negative in
every season. This is a statement about redundancy rather than about irrelevance,
and the three that lead the list are the three that overlap most: each of them
stands close to a restatement of the previous season's curve. A permutation
replaces one column with its own marginal distribution and leaves its correlated
partners in place, so
what it destroys is the combination the classifier had learned; a column that
duplicates information held elsewhere therefore reads as harmful when the
duplicate is removed. The figure makes the opposite point from the other side:
the scaling block permuted as a single unit raises the Brier score in five of
five seasons, by a median of $+0.049$ (range $0.035$ to $0.065$). Block and
member disagree, and correctly. That the block carries information when destroyed
does not make it an increment when added, and the ladder in
Table~\ref{tab:exceed} is the test that settles the question: adding it to M2
moves the Brier score by a median of
$+0.002$, with an interval excluding zero in one season of five. Redundancy with
$S_{t-1}$ reconciles the two readings, and it is the coupling of
Section~\ref{sec:coupling} seen from a third direction.

\subsection{Predicting epidemic size}
\label{sec:prediction}

The harder question is whether the tail helps forecast the magnitude of a season,
which is the quantity against which a health secretariat plans capacity. Here the
answer is negative, and the reason is the coupling measured in
Section~\ref{sec:coupling}.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation_results} reports the ladder over the five seasons at state
level. The figure that follows is not the municipal counterpart of that table:
Figure~\ref{fig:predobs} refits the same specification on the municipal panel, and
no municipal ladder for size is reported here. Against calibrated persistence,
the lagged-descriptor rung M1 carries
essentially no skill for size (median skill $0.30$, range $-0.06$ to $0.86$, positive in
3 of 5 seasons and with an interval excluding zero in 2 of 5), and
successive rungs do not improve on it: M2 has median $0.12$ and M3 $0.15$. The
differential of M3 against M2, the one that isolates
$(\alpha_{t-1}, x_{\min,t-1}, \dot\alpha_{t-1})$ and nothing else, shows no season in
which the block improves with an interval excluding zero, against the three the rule
requires; its interval excludes zero in 2 of the 5 seasons, both against the block,
with a median relative change in pinball loss at $\tau=0.5$ of $-5.2\%$.

The block was also expected to be most valuable at high $\tau$. The argument for
$\alpha_{t-1}$ is that it encodes how much of the risk lies in the tail, so that
any gain should concentrate at high $\tau$, and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}b was
constructed to test precisely that. It shows not a degradation but an absence of
signal. At $\tau=0.95$ the median differential is $+4.9\%$, with M3 ahead, and it
is positive in 4 of 5 seasons; but the intervals exclude zero in only 2 of 5, and those two point in \emph{opposite} directions, $+32.7\%$ in 2021 and
$-15.4\%$ in 2024. The same holds across the grid: the median of the per-season
differential moves between $-13.6\%$ and $+7.2\%$ with no trend, and no quantile
has three of the five seasons agreeing in favour of the block with an interval
excluding zero. At 2 of the 19 levels ($\tau=0.05$ and $\tau=0.25$) 3
seasons do exclude zero, and at both they agree that the block makes the
forecast worse. This is what the
coupling predicts. Both statistics are functions of the magnitude of the previous
season, so that the block is at best a second and noisier copy of $S_{t-1}$; the
ablation shows that the copy carries no usable increment, which leaves the upper
quantile where it was rather than displacing it in either direction.

\input{generated/tab_regression}

A skill score is a ratio and hides magnitude: it says that a rung is no better
than persistence without saying whether the miss is a hundred cases or a hundred
thousand. Table~\ref{tab:regression} measures the same forecasts on the case
scale, at the same loss. At $\tau=0.5$ the median forecast misses an annual total
by a median of $44{,}471$ cases under M1 and $54{,}242$ under M3, against
$64{,}089$ for calibrated persistence, and the corresponding median root mean
squared errors are $111{,}884$, $100{,}872$ and $117{,}728$. The scale-free
measure is harsher. MASE divides each absolute error by the mean absolute
one-step error the same unit showed in training, so a value above one means the
forecast is worse than carrying the previous season's count forward unchanged.
The best rung on that measure is M2, at $1.12$, and the MASE exceeds one in 4 of
the 5 seasons for every rung. The ladder beats the naive rule only in isolated
cells, 2021 for M1 at $0.72$ and 2025 for M2 at $0.68$.

The levels also show what a pooled average would hide. In the record season the median
absolute error runs from $2.99\times10^{5}$ to $3.28\times10^{5}$ cases across the
four rungs, against $1.52\times10^{4}$ to $6.71\times10^{4}$ in the 3 seasons
before it; the pinball losses for size are an order of magnitude larger in 2024 and
2025, $1.5$ to $1.7\times10^{5}$ cases, than in the other three, $1.2$ to
$3.2\times10^{4}$, because persistence extrapolated the record into a season that
fell back sharply. A pooled mean would therefore mostly describe how each rung
behaves across a change of regime, and we report the median across seasons and the
sign of the per-season differential instead.

That change of regime is the substantive lesson of this subsection, and it is
consistent with the descriptive result rather than in tension with it. If epidemic
size follows $\alpha \approx 2$, the largest season a municipality will experience
lies systematically outside the support of its observed history. A model that
already knows the size of the previous season is therefore approximately correct in
ordinary seasons and wrong by a large factor in the exceptional one, which is
exactly the pattern of the table and of Figure~\ref{fig:predobs}. The estimator
of Section~\ref{sec:regression} determines how that pattern is to be read, not the
pattern itself. Because it can produce a value
beyond its training responses, a large error in a record season is a statement
about the data, and the failure of the size forecast can be read as a finding
rather than as a property of the fit.

many alerts a season will generate.


The list itself can be counted, and counting it is what turns the ranking into a
decision. Table~\ref{tab:alarms} reads, in two ways, the list the classifier
produces in the record season. In 2024, 1{,}484 municipalities had the five seasons of
history needed to define their own upper quartile, and 1{,}032 of them exceeded it.

The first reading applies the declared rule, $p\ge 0.5$, unchanged. Rung M3
returns 648 alerts, 520 of them true and 128 false: four alerts in five are real,
at the price of missing half of the municipalities that did exceed. The
persistence benchmark alerts nobody, because the highest probability it assigns
anywhere in the season is $0.264$ --- the level problem of Figure~\ref{fig:map}
stated as an operational failure rather than as a calibration defect. Precision
must be read against the season it comes from: here $69.5\%$ of municipalities
exceeded their own upper quartile, so a service that alerted every one of them
would have been right $69.5\%$ of the time, and the list of 648 is better than
that by about ten percentage points.

The second reading takes the ordering and asks what a bounded list holds, which is
the form the decision takes when capacity, not probability, is what binds.
Following the first 600 municipalities of the M3 order yields 486 true
exceedances, against 417 in a list of the same length drawn without information;
M1, which carries neither the climate nor the scaling block, yields 437, and
ordering by population alone yields 394, fewer than chance. The advantage sits at
the head of the list: at 300 the M3 order holds 241, against 209 by chance, while
M1's holds 199, below chance. The list is therefore an order of attention. Its
length is an output of the rule and not a forecast of the season, and the gain
over no information is real but narrow, in line with the discrimination of
Figure~\ref{fig:roc}.



\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What each of the two exponents describes, and what neither describes}

The comparison across five constructions settles a question that the applied
literature has treated as empirical when it is largely definitional. What does not
survive is the inference from an exponent to a regime: the step that treats
$\alpha_W$ or $\alpha_S$ as a property of the pathogen or of the place, when it is
a property of the tuple (variable, sampling unit, dimension of pooling, comparison
model). Section~\ref{sec:verdicts} gives the range that inference has to span.

The same comparison identifies which construction \emph{does} carry a stable
tail: the epidemic size across municipalities within a season, power-law in all 16
national cross-sections and in 96\% of 353 state-year cross-sections, with the
lower bound in the hundreds of cases, two to three orders of magnitude above the
body of the distribution. The paper rests on this result.

The distinction between it and the weekly fits that collapse to
$x_{\min} \le 5$ is substantive rather than technical, and the reason is the one
given in Section~\ref{sec:size}: two analyses that agree on the exponent and
disagree on the lower bound are not estimating the same quantity. The two
exponents here happen to be nearly equal without measuring the same object.

\subsection{The tail as a risk specification rather than a forecast input}

The risk statement holds across the cross-section that produced it and stops one
season out, and both boundaries are worth fixing.

As a risk specification the fitted pair reproduces the empirical exceedance
frequency over two decades of epidemic size (Figure~\ref{fig:exceed}); the
distance is small by construction, the range over which it stays small is not. No
covariate, no training period and no fitted case count is involved: the statement
is available from the current cross-section, for every municipality, including
those that have never experienced an extreme season. For a service deciding how
much capacity to hold in reserve, this is the quantity the decision requires, and
a point forecast is not.

The reach is set by the season, not by the exponent. Within the season that
produced it the curve reproduces the data; one season ahead it holds at ten times
the bound and overstates a hundred times it. A fitted pair describes the
cross-section in which it was estimated, and it does not extend to an unobserved
season at an arbitrary threshold. That is a further reason why it is not a
forecast input: it gives a risk level for the current cross-section, which is what
a service holding capacity in reserve needs, and no information about which
municipality will have a large season next, which is what a forecast needs.

The predictive evaluation separates the two questions the pair could answer. Posed
as a probability --- will this municipality exceed its own upper quartile? --- the
answer is affirmative, but only where the panel can resolve it: the lagged
seasonal descriptors satisfy the pre-specified test at municipal level, and the
scaling block satisfies it at neither level. Posed as a magnitude, the answer is
negative, and the failure has a stated mechanism. The two are different products
for different decisions, and only the first is answered by this paper.

\subsection{How the coupling explains both results}

The coupling between $\alpha_S$ and $x_{\min}$ is the mechanism behind both halves
of the predictive result, and it is a finding rather than a caveat. Within municipalities the two estimates are
rank-correlated at $0.64$ (median over 24 states) and at $0.89$ nationally,
whereas a simulation with the true $\alpha_S$ held fixed at $2.0$ recovers
$-0.20$; the association is a property of the data and not of the estimator.

Descriptively, it makes the pair a compact readout of seasonal magnitude: two
numbers that move with the size of the season, vary far less than the case counts
they summarise (SD $0.150$ against a sampling noise of $0.03$), and are available
for every municipality. This is a substantive contribution to the characterisation
of a season, since it reduces a quantity spanning two orders of magnitude to a
stable low-dimensional summary.

Predictively, the same fact accounts for the null, and predicts it in advance.
$\alpha_S$ co-varies with the scale of the season and is, to that extent, a
re-encoding of $S_{t-1}$, which the model already contains. A re-encoding of a
variable already in the model must deliver no incremental information, so the
ablation was expected to find none, and none appears at any quantile of the grid.
The design has no power at the extreme quantile to sign the effect either way, and
reports the absence of a measured gain rather than a measured deterioration.

\subsection{The resolution limit of the size forecast}
\label{sec:smallmunic}

The aggregate panel of Figure~\ref{fig:predobs} pools municipalities that differ
by four orders of magnitude in population, and the pooling conceals a boundary
that a user of the forecast meets at once. Of the 4{,}664 municipalities with at
least one season in the platform's decomposition, 93.2\% have fewer than 100{,}000 inhabitants
and together carry 37.7\% of the cases in the series; 86.0\% have fewer than 50{,}000 and carry 25.3\%.
The median municipality has 13{,}220 inhabitants. Three limits set the resolution
of the forecast there, all of them arithmetic rather than epidemiological.

The first is the lower bound of the size distribution. The municipal power law is
fitted above $x_{\min}=300$ cases (Table~\ref{tab:main}), so the tail this paper
describes is the set of seasons above that threshold; 300 cases is an attack rate
of 2.3\% in the median municipality and of 0.3\% in one of 100{,}000 inhabitants. A small municipality therefore
enters the tail only in its exceptional seasons, which are precisely the seasons
the forecast is asked about, and it contributes few such points, so that the
$\alpha_S$ which would order its next season is estimated from a handful of
observations.

The second is the noise of the target. The annual total of a small municipality
is a small count, and a single imported case is a large relative change in it.
The third is that none of this is linear in population, and carrying population
as a covariate does not repair it. Where the exceedance label is referred to each
municipality's own history, a population-only reference reaches a median area
under the curve of $0.47$, indistinguishable from no skill, so the information
that separates a strong season from a weak one at that level is not the size of
the municipality.

The practical consequence is a change of product rather than of model. Below the
resolution limit the defensible output is the exceedance probability of
Section~\ref{sec:classifier} and the interval implied by the quantile grid, not a
point size; a point forecast there should be read as an order of magnitude. The
aggregate panel of Figure~\ref{fig:predobs} is the pooled view, and the four
state panels show what the forecast achieves where the limit does not bind.

\subsection{Relation to operational forecast systems}

Operational arbovirus platforms --- InfoDengue and Mosqlimate in Brazil, and the
national systems reviewed elsewhere --- already produce weekly municipal nowcasts
and short-horizon forecasts \citep{araujo2025large}. Alongside them, multivariate
machine-learning models have been fitted to municipal dengue series in Brazil,
comparing recurrent networks with random forest regression
\citep{mussumeci2020lstm}. The operational systems answer the question that this
paper finds difficult: what is happening now, and what will happen in the coming
weeks. The scaling description answers a question that they largely do not:
given the history of a municipality, how often should its cross-section produce a
season of a given size, expressed as a probability with two parameters. The two are
complementary, and the second is inexpensive enough to be computed for every
municipality in the country from a single cross-section.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound these conclusions.

\emph{The scaling block does not improve the size forecast.} This is reported in the
Results rather than only here because it is a measured outcome, but it is the
principal negative result of the paper and should accompany any use of it. Against
a benchmark that already carries the lagged case count, the block of
$(\alpha_{t-1}, x_{\min,t-1}, \dot\alpha_{t-1})$ fails the pre-specified test on
size in 0 of 5 seasons, and at the extreme quantile the design cannot sign the
effect in either direction. The mechanism is the coupling documented in
Section~\ref{sec:coupling}: a rung containing the scaling descriptors
\emph{alone}, without the lagged descriptors, would answer a different and
interesting question --- whether three power-law parameters can substitute for the
full set of covariates --- and is not reported here.

\emph{The municipal classifier is a ranking device, not a level forecast.} Its
discrimination reaches a median of $0.65$ only with the full ladder, against $0.58$
for the rung that satisfies the pre-specified test, and a season of a magnitude
outside the training record defeats every rung. The classifier orders
municipalities within a season of familiar magnitude; it does not anticipate a
change of regime, and in such a season its probabilities should not be read as
levels.

\emph{The case series are nowcast-adjusted.} The analysed variable is the estimated
case count produced by the nowcasting model of the platform, not the raw notification
count. This is the appropriate series for a retrospective description, because raw
notifications are incomplete in recent weeks, but the upper tail inherits whatever
smoothing the nowcast applies. If the nowcast shrinks extreme values, the fitted
exponents are biased towards the bulk, and the size distribution reported here is
somewhat less heavy than the notification distribution would be.

\emph{The state level is an arithmetic aggregate.} Where duration and peak timing
enter as covariates, the state values are the mean of municipal durations and the
median of municipal peaks. Neither is the duration or the peak of the aggregate
epidemic, which is the quantity a state health secretariat actually experiences. The
state-level results should be read as properties of the distribution of municipalities
within a state, not as properties of the epidemic of the state.

\emph{The cross-sections are short.} The national constructions rest on 16 annual
fits and the state constructions on three to 16 seasons per state, so that the
correlations reported in Section~\ref{sec:coupling} are estimated on small samples
even where the underlying cross-sections contain thousands of municipalities. The
within-state estimates are more robust than the national ones because they pool over
24 states.

\emph{The transport test rests on few pairs and unequal windows.} The out-of-season
comparison uses 14 consecutive pairs; the fifteenth is lost because the following
season does not carry enough municipalities above the fitted bound to be evaluated.
When $x_{\min}$ moves up the evaluated window narrows, so power is not uniform across
pairs, and because the test transports the pair as a whole it cannot attribute a
departure to the change in the exponent or to the change in the bound. What it buys
in exchange is the one thing the within-season agreement cannot provide: a
comparison that the estimator did not construct.

\emph{Extrapolation rests on an assumption rather than on data.} The primary
estimator continues the boundary trend of the spline basis linearly, which is what
allows it to name a season larger than any in its training window; but the value it
produces in that region is an extrapolation of a local slope, and it is only as
reliable as the assumption that the slope holds. In a simulation constructed at the
scale of this panel, with a season twice the previous one and training seasons of 10
to 1{,}000 cases, the model recovered the doubling to within 0.1\% at five times the
largest value in its training window and within 0.2\% at twenty times. This
establishes that the estimator recovers the relationship when the relationship is of
the assumed form; it does not establish that the relationship holds for dengue. The
defensible reading is that the estimator removes the ceiling on the magnitude the
forecast can take, so that a large error in a record season can be attributed to the
data, and that it does not remove the risk of a large error.

\emph{The classification threshold is a choice.} Exceeding the 75th percentile of a
municipality's own history defines a large season; at the 90th percentile the positive class
becomes small enough that the balanced accuracy of any classifier falls and the
comparison between rungs loses power. The threshold is therefore a design parameter,
fixed before the evaluation and reported with it.

\emph{The exponent cannot exceed three.} The estimator bounds $\alpha$ above by $3$,
so that a steeper tail is reported as $3$ rather than as its unconstrained maximum.
This is immaterial here: no national or state-year size fit approaches the bound, the
largest values being $2.94$ at state level and $2.25$ nationally, and it is
reached only by 12 of the 48{,}937 weekly fits, or $0.02\%$. Every exponent reported
in this paper is an unconstrained maximum.

\emph{Predictors are ecological and coarse.} No serotype composition, no
seroprevalence and no vector index enters the model, although all three are plausible
determinants of season size. None of the three is observable with the data at
hand: the attack ratio can be recovered from aggregated incidence only under strong
assumptions about the initial susceptible fraction, and serological surveys are what
would be needed to characterise serotype-specific immunity \citep{coelho2015attack};
where susceptibility has been built into municipal dengue forecasts it has improved
their weather-based predictions, which is reason to expect its omission to matter
here \citep{mcgough2021dynamic}. To the extent that these are stable within a municipality they
are absorbed by municipality-level effects; to the extent that they vary by season they
constitute unmodelled heterogeneity that the scaling block may be inadvertently
representing. This is a tenable explanation for the null reported here, and it is the
first that we would test.

\emph{The forecast has a resolution limit in population.} Below roughly
100{,}000 inhabitants the annual total is a small count dominated by a few
introductions, and no amount of covariate information recovers from it the size
of the next season; the argument and the magnitudes are in
Section~\ref{sec:smallmunic}. The four state panels of Figure~\ref{fig:predobs}
are selected from the states where that limit does not bind, and they are
therefore not a sample of national performance.

\subsection{Implications for surveillance practice}

Four practices follow, none of them costly, and they are the operational content of
the paper.

First, report the tuple. An exponent accompanied by its construction, its
$x_{\min}$, its sample size and its comparison model can be assessed by a reader;
an exponent alone cannot, and the five constructions examined here show that the
same exponent can carry opposite meanings. Name the variable as well: an
$\alpha_W$ and an $\alpha_S$ are not interchangeable, and the pair that a table
would read as agreement describes two objects a factor of $150$ apart in scale.
The convention is inexpensive and difficult to game, since $x_{\min}$ is selected
by the same goodness-of-fit criterion that selects the model and cannot be tuned
to produce a desired $\alpha$. We would argue that an exponent reported without
$x_{\min}$ should be treated as an incomplete statistic, in the same way that a
correlation is incomplete without its sample size.

Second, use the exceedance curve. The pair $(\alpha_S, x_{\min})$ fitted to the
current cross-section gives the probability that a municipality will exceed any
level, and the national fits reproduce the observed frequency of large seasons
over two decades. This is an inexpensive input to planning that requires no model
training and no history beyond the current season, and it is the only one of the
products here that a service can act on before a season begins.

Third, read the alert list as an ordering rather than as a level. The municipal
classifier ranks municipalities within a season of familiar magnitude, and the
ranking is what supports a bounded list: its length is an output of the decision
rule, not a forecast of how many alerts the season will generate. In a season of a
magnitude outside the training record the probabilities should not be read as
levels at all, and the ordering is all that survives.

Fourth, do not translate a heavy tail into a claim of unpredictability without
specifying the loss function. A distribution without a finite variance constrains
squared-error point forecasts and leaves probabilistic exceedance statements
intact, and the exceedance statement is usually the one a health service requires.

A claim that a scaling description improves forecasts is, in turn, meaningful only
against a specified benchmark, on a proper scoring rule, out of sample, with the
scaling block isolated from the blocks to which it is added, and with every channel
of leakage closed. The redundancy documented here would be invisible under a pooled
coefficient of determination without a benchmark, since between-municipality
differences are reproduced trivially by persistence.

\section{Conclusion}

Dengue epidemic size in Brazil follows a power law with an exponent close to two.
The law is preferred to an exponential in 16 of 16 national seasons and in 96\%
of 353 state-year cross-sections, and in each of those fits the lower bound lies
two to three orders of magnitude above the bulk, so that what is described is the
tail and not the body of small seasons. The same estimator applied to the weekly
count fails the identification instead: preferred in 77\% of the pooled municipal
series and in 23\% of the within-season ones, with a median lower bound of two
cases at both, so that the law describes the bulk and no tail.

Because the size exponent is close to two, the pair $(\alpha_S, x_{\min})$ fixes
the probability that a season exceeds any level $s$, and those two parameters
reproduce the observed frequency of extreme seasons over two decades. This is the
practical result of the paper: two numbers, taken from the current cross-section,
give the exceedance probability of any threshold for every municipality in the
country, without a training period and without a forecast.

The pair does not give the magnitude of the next season, and cannot. $\alpha_S$
co-varies with $x_{\min}$, and therefore with the scale of the season, at $0.89$
nationally and $0.64$ within states, so that the pair summarises how large a
season was and is a redundant input to any model that already contains the
previous season's size; the ablation confirmed the redundancy the coupling
predicted. What the pair adds instead is a probability, and the ladder shows the
difference between the two products: posed as a probability, the municipal
question of exceeding a unit's own upper quartile is answered by last season's
descriptors in 4 of 5 seasons, the only contrast in the study to satisfy the
pre-specified rule; posed as a magnitude, it is answered by no rung.

Two lessons generalise beyond this study. The first is a reporting one: an
exponent reported without its lower bound, its construction, its sample size and
its comparison model is not interpretable, since two analyses that agree on the
exponent can describe objects two orders of magnitude apart in scale, and the
convention costs one line in a table. The second is that the exceedance curve is a
deliverable rather than a by-product, because it is the only statement in this
paper that a service can act on before a season begins.

A heavy tail does rule out a point forecast of the largest event, and that part of
the received view is right. It does not rule out the probability that such an
event occurs, which two parameters estimate from a single cross-section, for every
municipality, including those that have never had an extreme season; and that
probability, not the magnitude, is what a decision to hold capacity in reserve is
made of. The obstacle to forecasting dengue size was never the shape of its
distribution. It was the choice of quantity.
