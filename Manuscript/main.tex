\begin{abstract}
Power-law tails are reported for dengue and read as a verdict on forecastability:
with an exponent near two, extremes cannot be anticipated. The fit supports a more useful
conclusion --- the tail prices the risk --- tested out of sample. On weekly surveillance
for 5{,}571 Brazilian municipalities (2010--2026) we fitted discrete power laws with
Kolmogorov--Smirnov $x_{\min}$ selection under five aggregations of one variable
(50{,}391 fits), compared with an exponential, on rolling origins.

The construction decides the verdict: median exponents span $1.95$ to $2.08$, and the
same counts support a power law in 77\% of municipalities pooled over a decade against
23\% in single seasons. Epidemic size survives every construction with a genuine tail:
median $\alpha=2.03$ across 16 national cross-sections and $1.98$ across 353
state-years, with $x_{\min}$ far above the bulk.

Because $\alpha\approx2$ fixes the exceedance curve, the pair is a two-parameter risk
statement calibrated against observation: 5.6--16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times
$x_{\min}$, and 0.3--2.7\% a hundred times it. The exponent reads seasonal magnitude,
rank-correlated with $x_{\min}$ at $0.89$ nationally and $0.64$ within states --- a coupling absent
in simulation.

Asked as exceedance, prediction is tractable where there are units to learn from: at
municipal level, with the label referred to each unit's own history, lagged descriptors
--- duration and peak timing among them --- beat calibrated persistence in 4 of 5
origins, the only contrast to clear the pre-registered rule, and AUC rises from
$0.50$ to $0.65$. At state level it resolves in only 2 of 5. The scaling block clears
nothing at either level, adding $+0.002$ Brier in median. For size it is indistinguishable
from zero: at $\tau=0.95$ the median differential is $+4.9\%$, positive in 4 of 5 origins
but excluding zero in only 2, in opposite directions. The tail belongs in a risk curve,
not a predictor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Understanding the dynamics of infectious disease epidemics has produced a very
prolific literature, both in mechanistic mathematical models and in statistical
ones \citep{siettos2013mathematical}. The former attempt to capture the causal
chain of transmission, the latter to make sense of a growing collection of
incidence data.

Among the most salient results of the mechanistic tradition is the basic
reproductive number $R_0$, which governs spreading speed and defines the
epidemic threshold. On the statistical side, most of the literature models
incidence as a stochastic process, usually Poisson or negative binomial
\citep{becker1999statistical, li2012log}. The central difficulty is
overdispersion: surveillance counts produce far more extreme observations than
any tractable count model comfortably allows.

A recurrent claim is that this overdispersion is not merely large but
\emph{scale-free}, that incidence follows a power law
\citep{rhodes1997critical, clauset2009power, neipel2020power}. The attraction is
that a very heavy tail implies extremes that most models fail to predict --- and
those extremes cause most of the disease and most of the economic burden. If
true, this bears directly on forecasting and on resource allocation.

The empirical record, however, is inconsistent. Reported exponents for dengue
range widely, some studies find exponential rather than power-law behaviour, and
the presence of a heavy tail appears to depend on the study period, the spatial
unit and the outcome variable. That heterogeneity has usually been attributed to
biology or geography: different places, or different years, are assumed to be
governed by different regimes.

We test a narrower and more useful set of claims. We begin from a different
premise than most of this literature: that a power law is not a verdict on
forecastability but a \emph{risk specification}. An exponent of two assigns a
definite probability to every exceedance level, so the fitted pair
$(\alpha,x_{\min})$ summarises the whole risk curve of a municipality --- and it
does so with two parameters, in a setting where the observables move by two
orders of magnitude between years. What a heavy tail denies is that the
\emph{magnitude} of the largest event can be estimated from the variance. What it
supplies is the probability that such an event occurs. Those are different
problems, and a proper scoring rule keeps them apart.

\paragraph{What this paper does.}
We ask three questions in sequence, each conditional on the previous one.

First, \emph{what is the exponent an exponent of?} Any epidemiological series can
be cut into distributions in several legitimate ways --- the weekly counts of one
city, the annual totals of one city, the set of epidemic sizes across cities in
one year --- and these are different random variables with different supports.
We fit the same estimator to the same data under five nested constructions and
report the exponent together with the diagnostic ($x_{\min}$) that makes it
interpretable. Epidemic size, the quantity surveillance services actually act
on, is the construction that survives. Duration and peak timing are not put
through that test at all: both are bounded by the length of the season, so a
scale-free law cannot apply to them a priori, and asking the question would
produce a verdict about the estimator rather than about the epidemic.

Second, \emph{what does the fitted pair buy?} If $(\alpha,x_{\min})$ is a risk
curve, it should reproduce the empirical frequency of large seasons. We test
that directly against the same cross-sections.

Third, \emph{does the tail improve our forecasts?} We pose the question in the
two forms in which it could plausibly succeed: as a classifier of whether a
municipality will exceed its historical upper quartile, which is the risk
statement itself; and as a quantile regression of epidemic size. Epidemic
duration and peak timing enter not as objects of a scaling claim, but as ordinary
covariates, since they are available before the season and may or may not help.
The evaluation is a rolling-origin design with a four-rung ablation ladder and a
decision rule fixed in advance.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A robust exponent for epidemic size, with the diagnostic that
makes it readable.} Under five nested constructions of the same variable on one
dataset, the two weekly-window constructions tell us what the diagnostic is for:
$x_{\min}$ collapses to five cases or fewer in 77\% of L1 and 81\% of L2 fits,
so the ``power law'' is being fitted to counts of one and two. The
cross-sectional constructions do not collapse, and they agree: $\alpha=2.03$ in
16 of 16 national cross-sections and 96\% of 353 state-years, with median
$x_{\min}$ of 730 and 169 cases respectively. We report $x_{\min}$ beside every
exponent, which is what separates the fits that describe a tail from the fits
that describe the data.

\item \textbf{The fitted pair as a calibrated risk curve.} Equation
\eqref{eq:exceed} turns $(\alpha,x_{\min})$ into $\Pr(S\ge s)$ for every $s$,
and the national fits reproduce the observed exceedance frequency over two
decades of epidemic size. This is a decision-relevant output that a point
forecast does not supply, and it costs two parameters.

\item \textbf{A measured coupling that says what the exponent is.} Within units,
$\alpha$ and $\log_{10}x_{\min}$ are rank-correlated at $0.64$ (median over 24 states) and at $0.89$ nationally, while a simulation with the true exponent fixed
recovers no such association. The pair is therefore a low-variance summary of
seasonal magnitude --- informative about size, but not independent of the lagged
case count, which is what a feature-based reading of it would require.

\item \textbf{An out-of-sample answer in the form the question can be answered.}
The probability of a large season is predictable where the panel is large enough
to resolve it: lagged descriptors improve the Brier score over calibrated
persistence in 4 of 5 municipal origins, the one contrast here to clear the
pre-registered rule, while at state level the same rung fails on 23--25 units per
origin. The scaling block adds no incremental skill to either task --- 0 of 5
origins for size, 1 of 5 for municipal exceedance --- and leaves the upper
quantile of the size forecast where it was. The scaling description earns its
place as a risk statement, not as a feature.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data sources}

Weekly dengue case counts were obtained from the InfoDengue/Mosqlimate
platform \citep{araujo2025large} for the period from 1 January 2010 to 22
January 2026 (the extraction date), for all Brazilian municipalities with
available data. The
variable used is the nowcast-adjusted estimated number of cases
(\texttt{casos\_est}), resampled to epidemiological weeks ending on Sunday
(\texttt{W-SUN}); zero-case weeks are retained in the series but excluded from
distributional fits, since the power-law family is defined on positive
support.

Annual epidemic descriptors were obtained from the EpiScanner module of the
same platform, which partitions each municipal series into epidemic episodes
and reports, for each episode, its total size (\emph{total cases}), its
duration in weeks (\emph{ep\_dur}) and the week of its maximum. Annual
cross-sections are available for the 16 years 2011--2026, the last of them
bounded by the extraction date of 22 January 2026; the pooled all-years fit is
stored separately (with a sentinel \texttt{year = 0}) and is excluded from all
year counts. This yields, for each state-year and for each national year, a
cross-section of municipal epidemics. Only the size is fitted as a power law;
\emph{ep\_dur} and the peak week enter the analysis as covariates of size.

Municipal population figures were taken from the estimates distributed with
the platform (\texttt{city\_stats} table; 5{,}571 municipalities, one with a
zero entry excluded). Population enters no power-law fit; it is used to compute
the descriptive correlations of Section~\ref{sec:verdicts} and, as
$\log_{10}$ population, as one predictor in the forecasting models of
Section~\ref{sec:targets}.

\subsection{Discrete power-law estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

For each sample we fit a discrete power law by maximum likelihood following
\citet{clauset2009power}. The fitted density for integer $x \ge x_{\min}$ is
%
\begin{equation}
p(x) = \frac{x^{-\alpha}}{\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})},
\end{equation}
%
where $\zeta(\alpha, x_{\min})$ is the generalised (Hurwitz) zeta function,
which normalises the discrete mass. The exponent $\alpha$ is estimated by
numerical maximisation of the likelihood, and the lower bound $x_{\min}$ is
selected jointly by minimising the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the
empirical and fitted cumulative distributions over all candidate values of
$x_{\min}$. Because the data are counts, the discrete formulation is used
throughout (\texttt{discrete=True}); the continuous approximation is not
employed at any level.

One property of the estimator has to be stated for the table to be read
correctly. The numerical fit constrains $\alpha$ to $(1,3]$: the upper limit of
the optimiser's default parameter range is $3$, so an estimate cannot exceed it
and a sample whose likelihood is maximised beyond that value returns $\alpha=3$
rather than its unconstrained maximum. The bound is immaterial for epidemic
size, which is the only variable fitted here. At the cross-sectional levels no
fit reaches it at all, the largest values being $2.94$ (L4) and $2.25$ (L6);
at the two weekly levels it is reached by 4 and 8 fits respectively, under
$0.08\%$ of each. The exponents reported in this paper are therefore
unconstrained maxima, not boundary values.

The choice of $x_{\min}$ is not a nuisance parameter. It determines which
fraction of the data the model actually describes, and therefore whether the
fitted exponent refers to a tail at all. We therefore retain $x_{\min}$ for
every fit and report its median and the proportion of fits with
$x_{\min}\le 5$ in each aggregation level.

\subsection{Model comparison}

A power law can always be fitted; the question is whether it describes the
data better than a plausible alternative. We compare each power-law fit
against an exponential distribution, which is the natural null for
epidemiological counts because it corresponds to a constant hazard of
termination and produces a light tail. The comparison follows the
likelihood-ratio procedure of \citet{clauset2009power}, as implemented in the
\texttt{powerlaw} package with \texttt{normalized\_ratio=True}.
%
\begin{equation}
R = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\ln p_{\mathrm{PL}}(x_i) - \ln p_{\mathrm{exp}}(x_i)\right],
\end{equation}
%
normalised so that its magnitude is comparable across samples of different
size. The sign of $R$ indicates the preferred model ($R>0$ favours the power
law, $R<0$ favours the exponential) and its associated $p$-value quantifies
whether the sign is distinguishable from sampling noise.

We adopt a deliberately conservative reporting convention. A level is
described as \emph{power-law supported} only when $R>0$ and $p<0.05$; as
\emph{exponential supported} only when $R<0$ and $p<0.05$; and as
\emph{inconclusive} otherwise. This prevents the common error of counting a
positive but non-significant $R$ as evidence for scaling.

\subsection{Identification diagnostic}

We flag a fit as \emph{tail-informative} when $x_{\min}$ exceeds the bulk of
the observed distribution, operationally $x_{\min}>5$ cases for weekly counts
and $x_{\min}$ above the median observed value for the aggregate levels. Where
$x_{\min}\le 5$, the fitted power law is being asked to describe the entire
empirical distribution, including the small-count regime where the discrete
power law is not a plausible generative model (case counts of 1 or 2 arise
from reporting mechanics, not from scale-free branching). Such fits are
retained in the database but are excluded from substantive interpretation, and
we report the proportion explicitly.

\subsection{From description to prediction}
\label{sec:bridge}

A fitted power law is not merely a description of a histogram; it is a
statement about exceedance probabilities. For integer $s \ge x_{\min}$,
%
\begin{equation}
\Pr(S \ge s) \;=\; \frac{\zeta(\alpha,s)}{\zeta(\alpha,x_{\min})},
\label{eq:exceed}
\end{equation}
%
so a single pair $(\alpha,x_{\min})$ parameterises the probability that a
municipality experiences an epidemic of at least any given size. Four
consequences of Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed} shape the forecasting design.

First, the exponent summarises \emph{risk} rather than \emph{activity}. Two
municipalities with the same expected annual incidence but different $\alpha$
face different probabilities of a catastrophic season, and that difference is
invisible to the mean. The fitted models quantify the claim rather than merely
asserting it: using the 16 national size fits, they imply that between 5.6\% and 16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times $x_{\min}$ in a given year, and
between 0.3\% and 2.7\% exceed one hundred times $x_{\min}$. Those are
falsifiable implications of the description, and Figure~\ref{fig:exceed}
confronts them with the empirical exceedance frequencies.

Second, $\alpha$ is a low-variance quantity \emph{relative to its target}.
Across the national cross-sections it takes values between $1.78$ and $2.25$, and it
is essentially unrelated to city size ($r=-0.043$). Annual case counts over the
same period vary by two orders of magnitude. The dispersion of $\hat\alpha$ is
not zero --- it exceeds sampling noise by about a factor of five in standard
deviation, and it co-varies with the selected $x_{\min}$
(Section~\ref{sec:coupling}) --- but it is small along precisely the dimension
in which the target is volatile. That is the specific reason to carry $\alpha$
into the predictive models rather than treating it as a descriptive curiosity,
and the same coupling is the reason the ablation, not the correlation, has to
decide the question.

Third, $\alpha\approx2$ has a direct methodological consequence for
evaluation. The second moment of a power law with $\alpha\le 2$ does not exist
in the continuous limit, and at $\alpha\approx2$ the sample mean is unstable,
so squared-error losses are decided by the few largest outbreaks. A model that
is good everywhere except in the tail can therefore look excellent or terrible
depending on which years fall in the test set. We consequently evaluate on
quantile (pinball) losses and on the classification of tail events, and treat
RMSE as a secondary, tail-dominated diagnostic.

Finally, the predictive hypothesis is stated in a form that can fail. If
$(\alpha,x_{\min})$ carries information about the coming season beyond what
lagged counts already encode, a model containing them should beat an otherwise
identical model without them, out of sample. If instead the exponent is close
to a deterministic function of the lower moments of the same series, the two
models should be indistinguishable --- and the descriptive result would then
carry no forecasting content. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} specifies the
comparison that decides between these outcomes, and
Section~\ref{sec:leakage} lists the three ways in which such a comparison could
be faked and how each is prevented.

\subsection{Predictive targets and feature construction}
\label{sec:targets}

The forecasting problem is posed at the level of an administrative unit
(municipality or state) and a target year $t$: every predictor is measured in
year $t-1$ or earlier, and the target is a property of the epidemic season of
year $t$. There is one target, epidemic size $S_t$ --- total cases in the annual
episode --- because that is the quantity surveillance services act on and the
only one of the three descriptors with unbounded support. Epidemic duration
$D_t$ (weeks) and peak timing $P_t$ (week of the seasonal maximum) are
predictors, not outcomes: they describe the phenology of the previous season and
the ladder asks whether that phenology helps forecast the next one's
magnitude.

Predictors fall into six blocks, listed in Table~\ref{tab:features}: lagged
epidemic metrics of the previous season, which include its duration and peak
timing; the scaling descriptors $(\alpha_{S,t-1},
x_{\min,S,t-1})$ from the power-law fit to the previous season; the historical
trend $\dot\alpha_{t-1}$, defined as the ordinary least-squares slope of
$\alpha_S$ over all years strictly before $t$ and set to zero where fewer than
two such years exist; the epidemiological driver $R_{0,t-1}$ reported by the
EpiScanner decomposition; $\log_{10}$ population; and eight quarterly climate
aggregates from year $t-1$, namely mean minimum temperature and mean minimum
humidity in each of Q1--Q4. Units lacking the mandatory lags (no previous
season) are dropped rather than imputed; the optional $\dot\alpha$ is set to
zero when undefined.

\subsection{Regression models}
\label{sec:regression}

Epidemic size is modelled on the log scale, $\log(1+S_t)$, because the target
spans four orders of magnitude and a model fitted on the raw scale would in
practice be fitted to the largest municipalities only; predictions are
returned to the case scale with $\exp(\cdot)-1$. That back-transformation is
biased downwards by Jensen's inequality, so a multiplicative factor
$c=\overline{S}_{\text{train}}\big/\overline{(\exp\hat y-1)}_{\text{train}}$ is
estimated on the training data and applied to the point predictions. The
correction is a mean-matching device, not a distributional one, and it is
deliberately not applied to the quantile models, whose losses are computed on
the back-transformed quantiles themselves.

The choice of estimator is not a tuning detail in this problem, and it is
stated as a design decision rather than left for the limitations. A tree
ensemble predicts a weighted average of the responses it was trained on, so it
cannot produce a value outside their range. Under $\alpha\approx2$ the season
of greatest public-health interest is precisely the one outside that range, so
an estimator bounded by its training responses is bounded exactly where the
question lives --- and a poor forecast from it cannot be interpreted, because
"the tail is unpredictable" and "this model cannot name the number" predict the
same loss. The primary estimator is therefore a quantile regression on a cubic
spline basis with linear boundary extrapolation
\citep{koenker1978regression}. It minimises the same pinball loss, so every
comparison in this paper is unchanged by the substitution; inside the observed
range the basis recovers the curvature a tree would provide; outside it the
boundary segment is continued linearly, which on the $\log(1+S)$ scale is a
multiplicative extrapolation on the case scale. The two predictors expressed in
cases, $S_{t-1}$ and $x_{\min,t-1}$, are logged before entering the basis,
without which the extrapolation is linear in the wrong variable. The $L_1$
penalty is $10^{-4}$, deliberately weak.

Three boosted alternatives are retained as a comparison arm, so that the
question "is it the estimator class?" can be answered by holding everything
else fixed: gradient boosting with quantile loss and piecewise-constant leaves,
the same with piecewise-linear leaves, and a histogram gradient-boosted model.
A random forest of 150 bootstrapped trees \citep{breiman2001random} appears
only in the illustrative scatter of Figure~\ref{fig:predobs}, where showing the
bounded prediction is the point of the panel. All quantile models are fitted on
the grid $\tau\in\{0.05,0.10,\dots,0.95\}$, which yields a full predictive
distribution per unit and year rather than a point forecast, and all stochastic
components use \texttt{random\_state=42}.

\subsection{Classification of large epidemics}
\label{sec:classifier}

The second predictive task is posed as a decision problem rather than an
estimation problem: will unit $i$ experience, in year $t$, a season larger than
the $p$-th percentile of its own historical distribution? The target is
%
\begin{equation}
Y_{i,t} \;=\; \mathbb{I}\!\left[\,S_{i,t} > Q_p\big(\{S_{i,k}\}_{k<t}\big)\,\right],
\end{equation}
%
with $p=0.75$ by default. The threshold is a \emph{within-fold} quantity: it is
recomputed from the training years of each fold and each split, never from the
full sample, so that the label of a test observation cannot depend on
information unavailable to the model. The same discipline governs the
class-imbalance weight, set to the negative-to-positive ratio of the training
fold.

The classifier is a penalised logistic regression on the same spline basis,
with the class imbalance handled by weighting the training rows by the
negative-to-positive ratio. The reason is the same as for the regressor: a
boosted classifier's predicted probability is a function of the base rates it
saw in training, so it cannot express "this season is far outside anything in
the panel". A logistic model extrapolates linearly in log-odds. Gradient-boosted
classifiers \citep{chen2016xgboost, ke2017lightgbm} are retained as the
comparison arm. Validation is an expanding-window split
defined on the year index. Fold $j$ trains on every unit-year whose year is
earlier than $v_j$ and validates on year $v_j$ alone, where the $v_j$ are the
$k=\min\{5,\,n_{\text{years}}-2\}$ most recent training years: a fold exists
only once at least two earlier years are available, and the number of folds is
capped at five. Because the folds are a function of the year vector and not of
row position, they are invariant to the order of the rows in the frame. Within
each fold the percentile threshold that defines the label, the class-imbalance
weight and the median imputer are all recomputed from that fold's training
years, so no quantity used to label or to weight a validation year can have
been influenced by it; a per-fold audit table (training window, held-out year,
fold sizes, threshold, weight) is retained with the results.

This distinction is not cosmetic, and it is the one step of this pipeline whose
failure is silent. An implementation that applies a contiguous-index split to a
frame sorted by unit rather than by year produces a grouped-by-municipality
split: each fold trains on some municipalities over the whole calendar and
validates on others over the same calendar, so validation contains no year that
is absent from training. On the training panel used here --- 21{,}943 rows,
3{,}976 municipalities, 2012--2025 --- all five folds of such a split train on
2012--2025 and validate on 2012--2025, and 100\% of validation rows carry a
year already present in training. The quantity being estimated is then
transportability to new municipalities, not temporal generalisation, and the
estimate depends on an undeclared sort order: merely reordering the rows of a
synthetic benchmark moved its cross-validated AUC from 0.813 to 0.835, whereas
the year-indexed split returns the same folds and the same AUC under every
permutation of the rows. The trap is narrower but not absent when the frame
happens to be year-sorted, because contiguous blocks then still cut through
calendar years. The splitter therefore takes the year vector explicitly, and
\texttt{assert\_forward\_in\_time} raises rather than warns when any
validation year is not strictly later than the training years of its fold. The decision threshold applied to the predicted
probability is chosen by Youden's $J$ statistic \citep{youden1950index},
computed on the out-of-fold predictions rather than on the training fit, and
then held fixed for the test set. We report the area under the ROC curve, the
area under the precision--recall curve (the more informative summary under
class imbalance), the Brier score \citep{gneiting2007strictly}, the $F_1$ score
at the Youden threshold and a calibration curve, and we test differences in AUC
between nested feature sets by the method of \citet{delong1988comparing}.
Feature attribution is measured by held-out permutation importance on the
Brier score, reported both per predictor and for the scaling block shuffled
as a unit \citep{breiman2001random}: under the collinearity that
Section~\ref{sec:coupling} documents, a per-column ranking alone
understates a block whose members can stand in for one another.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and ablation ladder}
\label{sec:ablation}

A single temporal split cannot distinguish a feature that helps from a feature
that happened to help once. We therefore adopt a rolling-origin design: for
each forecast origin $y\in\{2021,\dots,2025\}$ the model is trained on all
observations with year $<y$ and evaluated on year $y$, and the per-origin
losses are pooled. This gives five honest out-of-sample years and allows skill
to be read as a function of the history available to the model.

Within each origin, the incremental value of the scaling description is
isolated by the ablation ladder of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. Each rung adds one
block of predictors to the previous one, so a contrast between consecutive
rungs is attributable to the block that was added. The rung of substantive
interest is M2 versus M3, which differ \emph{only} in the presence of
$(\alpha_{S,t-1},x_{\min,S,t-1},\dot\alpha_{t-1})$.

Operationally, M0 forecast values are scaled by the empirical $\tau$-quantile of
the within-unit year-on-year ratio, estimated on the training years, so that the
same benchmark can be evaluated at every quantile level rather than only at the
median. At $\tau=0.5$ the scale factor is close to unity and M0 reduces to plain
persistence; at the other levels this is the honest analogue of persistence,
not a straw man.

M0 is deliberately naive, because a benchmark that is already sophisticated
manufactures skill for whatever is added to it. Skill is reported as
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SS} \;=\; 1-\frac{L(\text{model})}{L(\mathrm{M0})},
\end{equation}
%
on the primary loss of each task --- the pinball loss at $\tau=0.5$ for each
of the three regression targets, and the Brier score for the classifier, both
proper scoring rules
\citep{gneiting2007strictly}. Secondary losses are MAE and RMSE of the point
forecasts, the mean absolute scaled error of \citet{hyndman2006another} to make
errors comparable across units of very different size, and the pinball loss at
each $\tau$ on the grid.

Because units differ by orders of magnitude in scale, all model-versus-model
comparisons are made on paired loss differentials computed within the same unit
and year; their uncertainty is estimated by a bootstrap that resamples
\emph{units} rather than observations, preserving the within-unit correlation
across years, with 2{,}000 replicates. For the pooled comparison across origins
we additionally report the Diebold--Mariano statistic
\citep{diebold1995comparing} with a Newey--West correction, since the loss
series are serially correlated by construction.

The decision rule is fixed in advance. The scaling block is judged to add
predictive value only if M3 improves on M2 on the primary loss with a 95\%
bootstrap interval for the paired difference that excludes zero, in the same
direction, for at least three of the five forecast origins. This is stricter
than a single significant pooled test, and deliberately so: a pooled test can
be carried by one anomalous season, which is exactly the failure mode that a
heavy-tailed target invites.

\subsection{Leakage controls}
\label{sec:leakage}

Four sources of leakage are structurally possible in this design, and each is
closed explicitly.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Contemporaneous scaling descriptors.} $\alpha_{S,t}$ is estimated
from the weekly counts of season $t$, which are part of the target. Only
$\alpha_{S,t-1}$ enters as a predictor, and $\dot\alpha_{t-1}$ is computed
exclusively from years strictly before $t$.

\item \textbf{The classification threshold.} $Q_p$ is computed inside each fold
from training years only. Computing it once on the full sample would leak the
distribution of the test years into the labels of the training years, inflating
apparent skill by construction.

\item \textbf{Cross-sectional information.} The cross-section of municipalities
within year $t$ is an outcome of season $t$ and is therefore never a predictor;
the scaling descriptors always come from the previous season's cross-section.

\item \textbf{The validation split itself.} Folds are defined on the year index
(Section~\ref{sec:classifier}), so no fold trains on a year at or after the
year it validates on. This is the only one of the four channels that fails
silently in a plausible implementation --- every number still looks like a
respectable cross-validation score --- which is why it is asserted in code
rather than merely documented.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Software and reproducibility}

All fits are stored in a single SQLite database
(\texttt{powerlaw\_results.db}) committed to the project repository, together
with the fitting code (\texttt{fitpl.py}) and the cached parquet extracts of
every model result. Every number in this manuscript is derived from that
database by the queries in Appendix~\ref{app:verify}; no statistic is
transcribed by hand. The full pipeline is therefore re-runnable, and a reader
can confirm or contradict each figure with a single SQL statement.

\subsection{The aggregation level decides the verdict}

to the 23\% of L2. Median exponents span $1.95$ to $2.08$ and selected lower
bounds run from a single case to about $4\times10^{4}$, four orders of
magnitude. Nothing distinguished the analyses except the construction.

\subsection{Epidemic size is power-law with an exponent of two}
\label{sec:size}

The construction that survives both tests is the one that matters
operationally: the distribution of epidemic \emph{size} across units within a
year. At the national cross-section (L6) all 16 annual fits favour the power law
and all are significant, with $\alpha=2.03$ (IQR 1.90--2.10) and $x_{\min}=730$ cases. The exponent is remarkably stable, ranging only from 1.78 to 2.25 over sixteen years, in a period in which annual case counts vary by two orders of
magnitude. At state level (L4) the picture is consistent but weaker in
significance, as expected from smaller cross-sections: the power law is preferred
in 96\% of the 353 state-year fits, significant in 52\%, with median $\alpha=1.98$ and median $x_{\min}=169$ cases.

This is the object the rest of the paper works with, and it is worth being
precise about why. The median lower bound of 730 cases at national level and 169
at state level places the fit well above the bulk of a distribution whose
observations span four orders of magnitude: what is being described is the upper
region where the risk sits, not the body of small seasons. The empirical
complementary cumulative distribution of Figure~\ref{fig:ccdf} shows the same
thing graphically --- a straight line over two decades above $x_{\min}$.

\subsection{What the exponent is: a readout of the scale of the season}
\label{sec:coupling}

The fitted pair is not a pair of independent coefficients, and the direction of
that dependence determines how the description can be used.

the levels where it can be measured, together with the association recovered

The association is strong at national level and strong \emph{within} states over
time --- positive in 92\% of the 24 states with at least six years of data, and
significantly positive in 67\% --- but weak between states. It is therefore a
within-unit, over-time relationship, not a contrast between places. The control
recovers no such association, so the estimator does not manufacture it: it places
the sampling dispersion of $\hat\alpha$ at $0.03$ (40 replicates, samples of
800--4{,}000 points, true exponent fixed at $2.0$), against an observed standard deviation of $0.150$ across the 16 national fits. The year-to-year movement in
$\alpha$ is real, roughly five times the sampling noise in standard deviation,
even though it remains small next to the variation in the case counts themselves.

Read as description, this is the most informative single result about
$(\alpha,x_{\min})$ in the paper. Because $x_{\min}$ is measured in cases, it
tracks the magnitude of the season: years in which municipalities produce large
epidemics place the cut higher, and the exponent moves with it. The pair is
therefore a two-number summary of how large a season was --- one that is available
for every municipality, is far less volatile than the case count it summarises,
and is stable enough to be compared across years (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}).
Read as a feature set, the same fact is a warning: an exponent that co-varies
with the scale of the season is, to that extent, a re-encoding of $S_{t-1}$,
which any model of size already carries. Both readings are correct, and they
predict different things about the ablation in Section~\ref{sec:prediction}.

The exponent is essentially unrelated to city size
($\mathrm{corr}(\alpha,\log_{10}\text{population})=-0.043$, Spearman $-0.048$;
$n=5{,}264$) and only weakly related to incidence (Pearson $-0.143$, Spearman $-0.248$; $n=4{,}663$). Larger or more heavily burdened municipalities are not in
a different scaling regime: the exponent is a property of the construction, and
within a unit of the scale of the season.

\subsection{The fitted law as a risk curve}
\label{sec:riskmodel}

Equation~\eqref{eq:exceed} makes the description operational. A power law with
exponent $\alpha$ and lower bound $x_{\min}$ assigns
$\Pr(S\ge s)=\zeta(\alpha,s)/\zeta(\alpha,x_{\min})$ for every level $s$, so the
fitted pair is a complete statement about how often extreme seasons occur. It
requires no fitted case count, no covariate, and no training period: it is
available from the current cross-section alone.

Figure~\ref{fig:exceed} confronts the exceedance curve implied by each national
fit with the empirical exceedance frequency of the same cross-section. The
agreement holds over roughly two decades of epidemic size. Numerically, the 16
national fits imply that between 5.6\% and 16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten
times $x_{\min}$ in a given year, and between 0.3\% and 2.7\% exceed a hundred
times it. Those are statements a surveillance service can act on and a
subsequent season can falsify.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig09_exceedance}
\caption{The fitted power law as a probabilistic statement about exceedance. For
each national cross-section, curves give
$\Pr(S\ge s)=\zeta(\alpha,s)/\zeta(\alpha,x_{\min})$ evaluated at the fitted

This is the sense in which the description is predictive before any machine
learning is involved: $\alpha$ is not a summary of central tendency but a
summary of how much of the risk sits in the tail, and it is available for every
municipality even when that municipality has never experienced an extreme
season. The familiar inference --- that a divergent second moment makes extreme
seasons unpredictable --- is a statement about squared-error loss, not about
information. Prediction of the tail event and prediction of its size are
different problems.

\subsection{Predicting the probability of a large epidemic}
\label{sec:classresults}

The exceedance formulation has a direct predictive counterpart: rather than
asking how large the season will be, ask whether it will fall in the upper
quartile of the unit's own history. This is the risk statement itself, and it is
the natural place to look for value in the scaling descriptors, since a tail
exponent is by construction a statement about upper-tail probabilities.

We posed it at the two levels where it is asked operationally. At state level
(Table~\ref{tab:exceed}) lagged epidemic descriptors --- which include the
previous season's duration and peak timing as ordinary covariates, alongside
size, $R_0$ and population --- move the Brier score in the expected direction:
median $+0.126$, positive in 4 of 5 origins, by up to $0.212$. The intervals
exclude zero in only 2 of the 5 origins, against the 3 the rule requires, so the
rung fails the rule at state level: each origin is a bootstrap over 23--25 units
and the median interval is $0.26$ wide, wider than the effect it is asked to
resolve. Climate adds nothing (median of $+0.027$) and neither does the scaling
block (median of $+0.000$, 0 of 5). Per origin the M1$-$M0 differentials are $-0.064$, $+0.074$, $+0.126$, $+0.212$ and $+0.149$ for 2021 to 2025 --- negative
in the first origin and positive thereafter, which is what a model that needs
history to be trained on should show.

At municipal level the same ladder runs over 3{,}976 municipalities with enough
history for a within-unit threshold (at least five seasons), with between 580 and
1{,}484 units evaluated per origin. Two things change, and only one of them is
about the scaling descriptors. The first is a correction. With the threshold
taken from each municipality's own seasons rather than pooled across the country,
the population-only reference falls to a median AUC of $0.47$ (range
$0.454$--$0.522$), against $0.94$ when the threshold is pooled: the pooled label
was measuring municipality size, and the within-unit label is not. The second is
that the lagged block, which the state level could not resolve, clears the rule
here. Its Brier differential is a median of $+0.049$, positive in 4 of 5 origins
and excluding zero in 4 of 5 origins --- the only contrast in this paper to satisfy the pre-registered rule (Table~\ref{tab:exceed}). Discrimination rises from
$0.50$ for the no-skill reference to $0.58$, $0.63$ and $0.65$ for M1, M2 and M3;
M3 is best of the three in 3 of 5 origins and gains a median $+0.013$ over M2.

The inversion between the two levels is a statement about precision, not about
epidemiology. The state estimate of the same effect is the larger of the two in
absolute terms ($+0.126$ against $+0.049$) and the less certain by a factor of
eight in interval width ($0.26$ against $0.034$), so a rule that fails at one
level and passes at the other is failing on sample size: twenty-five units per
origin cannot resolve an effect that 1{,}484 can. What the two levels agree on is
the ranking that matters for this paper. The scaling block clears the bar at
neither level: it moves in the right direction in 5 of 5 origins at municipal
level, by a median of $+0.002$, but its interval excludes zero in only one of
them, and at state level in none. Whether a municipality will have an exceptional
season is partly predictable from how large the last one was; the exponent does
not add to that.

One origin deserves a flag. In 2024, 69.5\% of the evaluated municipalities
exceeded their own upper quartile, against a training base rate of 26.4\%. The
no-skill reference scores a Brier of $0.40$ there and the fitted rungs about $0.25$
--- better than the reference, but worse than the $0.21$ a predictor that
knew the year's own frequency would earn. That season was a national record, and
no model trained on earlier seasons anticipated its level. This is the honest
boundary of the exercise: the classifier ranks units within a season of familiar
magnitude; it does not foresee a regime break.

Figures~\ref{fig:roc} and~\ref{fig:importance} report discrimination, calibration
and attribution for the municipal classifier. The calibration panel is the one to
read: a classifier that is useful for planning must track the diagonal in the
upper deciles, not merely rank well on average.

\subsection{Predicting epidemic size}
\label{sec:prediction}

The harder question is whether the tail helps forecast the magnitude of a
season, which is what a health secretariat orders beds against. Here the answer
is negative, and the reason is the coupling measured in
Section~\ref{sec:coupling}.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation_results} reports the ladder over the five origins at
state level. Against calibrated persistence, the lagged-metrics rung M1 carries
essentially nothing for size (median skill $0.30$, range $-0.06$ to $0.86$,
positive in 3 of 5 origins and 2 of 5 with an interval excluding zero), and
successive rungs do not improve on it: M2 has median $0.12$ and M3 $0.15$. The
M3$\to$M2 contrast --- the one that isolates $(\alpha_{t-1},x_{\min,t-1},
\dot\alpha_{t-1})$ and nothing else --- excludes zero in 0 of 5 origins against
the 3 required, with a median relative change in pinball loss at $\tau=0.5$ of $-5.2\%$.

\input{generated/tab_ablation}

The block was also expected to be worth most at high $\tau$. The argument for
$\alpha_{t-1}$ is that it encodes how much of the risk sits in the tail, so any
gain should concentrate at high $\tau$, and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}b was drawn
to test precisely that. What it shows is not a degradation but an absence of
signal. At $\tau=0.95$ the median differential is $+4.9\%$ --- M3 ahead --- and
it is positive in 4 of 5 origins, but the intervals exclude zero in only 2 of 5,
and those two point in \emph{opposite} directions ($+32.7\%$ in 2021, $-15.4\%$
in 2024). The same holds across the grid: the median of the per-origin
differential moves between $-13.6\%$ and $+7.2\%$ with no trend, and there is no
quantile at which 3 of 5 origins agree in direction with an interval excluding
zero --- 0 of 19 quantiles clear the bar. This is what the coupling predicts. Both
statistics are functions of the magnitude of the previous season, so the block is
at best a second and noisier copy of $S_{t-1}$; the ablation shows that the copy
carries no usable increment, which leaves the upper quantile where it was rather
than moving it in either direction.

Two origins dominate the size column and need separate mention. The 2024 season
was the largest on record and 2025 fell back sharply; persistence extrapolated
the record into 2025, and in absolute terms lost there much of what it had gained
in 2024. The pinball losses for size are accordingly an order of magnitude larger
in those two origins ($1.5$--$1.7\times10^{5}$ cases) than in the other three
($1.2$--$3.2\times10^{4}$), so a pooled mean over origins would mostly describe
how each rung behaves across a regime shift. We therefore report the median
across origins and the sign of the per-origin differential, not a pooled
average.

That regime shift is the substantive lesson of this subsection, and it is
consistent with the descriptive result rather than in tension with it. If
epidemic size follows $\alpha\approx2$, the largest season a municipality will
experience is systematically outside the support of its observed history. A
model that already knows the previous season's size is therefore approximately
right in ordinary years and wrong by a large factor in the exceptional one ---
which is exactly the pattern of the table, and of Figure~\ref{fig:predobs}.
What the estimator choice of Section~\ref{sec:regression} changes is the
interpretation of that pattern, not the pattern. With a model that cannot
produce a number outside its training responses, a large error in a record year
is uninformative, because it is what the estimator must do. With one that can,
the error is a statement about the data, and the failure of the size forecast
can be read as a finding rather than as an artefact of the fit.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the exponent is, and what it is not}

The five-level comparison settles a question that the applied literature has
treated as empirical when it is largely definitional. Median exponents span
$1.95$ to $2.08$ for the same variable, the share of fits favouring a power law
ranges from 23\% to 100\% across constructions, and a single change of temporal
window moves support from 77\% to 23\% with no transmission parameter
altered. The step that
does not survive is the inference from an exponent to a regime: the step that
treats $\alpha$ as a property of the pathogen or the place, when it is a property
of the tuple $(\text{unit},\ \text{window},\ \text{variable},\ \text{comparison
model})$.

But the same comparison identifies which construction \emph{does} carry a stable
tail. Epidemic size across units within a year is power-law at every level where
$x_{\min}$ lies above the bulk: $\alpha=2.03$ in 16 of 16 national cross-sections, 96\% support across 353 state-years, and a lower bound in the
hundreds of cases, two to three orders of magnitude above the body of the
distribution. That is not a fragile result, and it is the one worth building on.
The distinction between this and the weekly-count fits that collapse to
$x_{\min}\le5$ is not a technicality: at $x_{\min}=2$ the fitted law describes
counts of one and two cases, and at $x_{\min}=730$ it describes the upper decade
of a distribution. Two analyses reported as agreeing on the exponent can be
describing entirely different objects --- L1 and L3 have median exponents of
$1.95$ and $2.08$, a difference of $0.13$, while their median lower bounds differ
by a factor of $150$.

\subsection{The tail is a risk specification, not a forecast input}

The most consequential move in this paper is to stop treating $\alpha\approx2$ as
a verdict on forecastability and treat it as a probability statement. A power law
assigns $\Pr(S\ge s)$ at every level; what it denies is that the \emph{magnitude}
of the largest event can be estimated from the variance. Those are different
claims, and conflating them is what produced the intuition that heavy-tailed
epidemics are unforecastable.

Read as a risk specification, the fitted pair performs well and cheaply.
Two parameters reproduce the empirical exceedance frequency over two decades of
epidemic size (Figure~\ref{fig:exceed}), and they imply that between 5.6\% and 16.5\% of municipalities exceed ten times $x_{\min}$ in a given year against
0.3--2.7\% exceeding a hundred times it. No covariate, no training period and no
fitted case count is involved: the statement is available from the current
cross-section, for every municipality, including those that have never
experienced an extreme season. For a service deciding how many beds to hold in
reserve, that is closer to the decision than a point forecast is.

The predictive evaluation confirms the division of labour, and it locates it
more sharply than a single level could. Where the question is posed as a
probability --- will this municipality exceed its own upper quartile? --- the
answer is yes, but only where the panel can resolve it. At municipal level lagged
epidemic descriptors improve the Brier score over calibrated persistence in 4 of 5
origins, with the interval excluding zero in each of them, and lift
discrimination from $0.50$ to $0.65$ AUC. At state level the same rung produces a
larger point estimate and fails the rule, on 23--25 units per origin. The scaling
block, which is what the paper is about, clears the bar at neither level. Where
the question is posed as a magnitude, the answer is no, and the failure is
orderly rather than mysterious.

\subsection{Why the coupling explains both results}

The coupling between $\alpha$ and $x_{\min}$ is the mechanism behind both halves
of the predictive result, and it deserves to be read as a finding rather than as
a caveat. Within units the two estimates are rank-correlated at $0.64$ (median over 24 states) and at $0.89$ nationally, while a simulation with the true
exponent held fixed at $2.0$ recovers $-0.20$. The association is a property of
the data, not of the estimator.

Descriptively, that makes the pair a compact readout of seasonal magnitude: two
numbers that move with the size of the season, vary far less than the case counts
they summarise (SD $0.150$ against a sampling noise of $0.03$), and are available
for every unit. This is a real contribution to how a season can be characterised
--- it is a shrinkage of a two-order-of-magnitude quantity into a stable
low-dimensional summary.

Predictively, the same fact is the explanation for the null. An exponent that
co-varies with the scale of the season is, to that extent, a re-encoding of
$S_{t-1}$, which the model already has. The ablation measures exactly that
redundancy: the scaling block adds nothing to a rung that already knows last
season's size (0 of 5 origins), and at $\tau=0.95$ its differential is
$+4.9\%$ in median with intervals that exclude zero in only 2 of 5 origins and
in opposite directions, so the test has no power there to sign the effect either
way. The redundancy was predictable from the coupling before the ablation was run,
and the ablation confirmed it where the prediction had content: a re-encoding of a
variable already in the model must deliver no incremental information, and none
appears at any quantile of the grid. What the earlier draft of this paper claimed
on the same data --- that the block actively damages the upper quantile --- does
not survive the estimator change, and we withdraw it.

It also explains why the exceedance question behaves differently. Asking whether
a season will be large is a question about upper-tail probability, and the
coefficient on the lagged descriptors there is doing the work; adding a
transformation of the same lagged quantity cannot improve it, which is why the
scaling block's Brier differentials sit at $\pm0.01$ with no interval excluding
zero. The information the exponent carries is real, and it is already in the
lagged count.

\subsection{Size is the hard target, and that is consistent with the description}

Nothing in this paper shows that epidemic size can be forecast well. Against
calibrated persistence no rung achieves skill in more than 2 of 5 origins, and
two of the five origins dominate the loss because they straddle a regime shift:
2024 was the largest season on record and 2025 fell back sharply.

It would be convenient to attribute that to the estimator, and an earlier draft
of this paper did. The attribution does not hold. Tree ensembles predict within
the support of their training responses, so under a heavy tail the season of
greatest public-health interest sits systematically outside it; but the primary
model here is a spline-basis quantile regression, which by construction continues
the boundary trend past the largest response it was trained on
(Figure~\ref{fig:predobs}). A record year is therefore a statement about the data
rather than an artefact of a fit that cannot leave its support. The quantile
regression at $\tau=0.95$ represents the upper region instead of interpolating
inside it, and the forecast still fails in 2024: knowing last season's size and
duration, its peak week, the climate covariates and the scaling descriptors does
not order five origins at 23--25 units each.

The honest summary is therefore a division of labour rather than a verdict. The
tail does not tell you how big next season will be; it tells you how likely an
extreme season is, and it does so with two parameters and no training data. Those
are different products for different decisions, and the second is the one a
surveillance service can use before the season starts.

\subsection{A reporting convention}

The practical consequence of the descriptive half is a convention, and it is
cheap to adopt. An exponent should travel with the lower bound that produced it,
the unit of analysis, the number of observations, and the alternative model it
was tested against. Of these, $x_{\min}$ is the most informative and the most
frequently omitted, because it separates two analyses that look identical in a
table of results. The diagnostic is easy to apply and hard to game, since
$x_{\min}$ is chosen by the same goodness-of-fit criterion that selects the model
and cannot be tuned to produce a desired $\alpha$. We would go further: an
$\alpha$ reported without $x_{\min}$ should be treated as an incomplete
statistic, in the same way that a correlation is incomplete without its sample
size.

We would add a second convention, specific to the argument this paper tests. A
claim that a scaling description improves forecasts is only meaningful against a
specified benchmark, on a proper scoring rule, out of sample, with the scaling
block isolated from the blocks it is added to, and with every leakage channel
closed. The redundancy documented here would have been invisible under a pooled
$R^2$ against no benchmark: an earlier run of the same pipeline reported an
out-of-bag $R^2$ of $0.35$ for size, computed on the pooled cross-section on the
log scale, which reflected between-unit differences that persistence reproduces
trivially.

\subsection{Relation to operational forecast systems}

Operational arbovirus platforms --- InfoDengue and Mosqlimate in Brazil, and the
national systems reviewed elsewhere --- already produce weekly municipal
nowcasts and short-horizon forecasts \citep{araujo2025large}. Those systems
answer the question this paper finds hard: what is happening now, and what will
happen in the next few weeks. The scaling description answers a question they
mostly do not: given this municipality's history, how often should its
cross-section produce a season of a given size, expressed as a probability with
two parameters. The two are complementary, and the second is cheap enough to be
computed for every municipality in the country from a single cross-section.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations bound these conclusions.

\emph{The scaling block does not improve the size forecast.} We state this in the
Results rather than only here because it is a measured outcome, but it is the
principal negative result of the paper and should travel with any use of it.
Against a benchmark that already carries the lagged case count, the block of
$(\alpha_{t-1},x_{\min,t-1},\dot\alpha_{t-1})$ fails the pre-registered test on
size in 0 of 5 origins. At the 95th percentile its differential is $+4.9\%$ in
median, with intervals excluding zero in only 2 of 5 origins and in opposite
directions, so this design cannot sign the effect there: with 23--25 units per
origin the test is underpowered at the extreme quantile, and we report the
absence of a measured gain rather than a measured harm. The mechanism is the
coupling documented
in Section~\ref{sec:coupling}. A rung containing the scaling descriptors
\emph{alone}, without the lagged metrics, would answer a different and
interesting question --- whether three power-law parameters can substitute for the
full covariate stack --- and is not reported here.

\emph{The municipal classifier is a ranking device, not a level forecast.} With the
exceedance label referred to each municipality's own history, the population-only
reference falls to a median AUC of $0.47$, so the task no longer measures unit
size, and the lagged block clears the pre-registered incremental test at that
level. What bounds the claim is what the classifier is for: discrimination
reaches a median $0.65$, the scaling block adds a median $+0.002$ Brier, and the
2024 origin --- in which 69.5\% of units exceeded their own upper quartile against
a training base rate of 26.4\% --- defeats every rung. The classifier orders
municipalities within a season of familiar magnitude. It does not anticipate a
regime change, and in a record year its probabilities should not be read as
levels.

\emph{The case series are nowcast-adjusted.} The variable analysed is the
estimated case count produced by the platform's nowcasting model, not the raw
notification count. This is the right series for a retrospective description,
because raw notifications are incomplete in recent weeks, but it means that the
upper tail inherits whatever smoothing the nowcast applies. If the nowcast
shrinks extreme values, the fitted exponents are biased towards the bulk, and the
size distribution reported here is somewhat less heavy than the notification
distribution would be.

\emph{The state level is an arithmetic aggregate.} Where duration and peak
timing enter as covariates, the state values are the mean of municipal durations
and the median of municipal peaks. Neither is the duration or the peak of the
aggregate epidemic, which is the quantity a state health secretariat actually
experiences. The state-level results should be read as properties of the
municipality distribution within a state, not as properties of the state
epidemic.

\emph{The cross-sections are short.} The national levels rest on 16 annual fits
and the state levels on three to 16 years per state, so the correlations reported
in Section~\ref{sec:coupling} are estimated on small samples even where the
underlying cross-sections contain thousands of municipalities. The within-state
estimates are more robust than the national ones because they pool over 24
states.

\emph{Extrapolation is bought with an assumption, not with data.} The primary
estimator continues the boundary trend of the spline basis linearly, which is
what lets it name a season larger than any in its training window; but the
number it produces in that region is an extrapolation of a local slope, and it
is only as good as the assumption that the slope holds. In a synthetic check
built at the scale of this panel --- a season twice the previous one, fitted on
previous seasons of 10 to 1{,}000 cases --- the model recovered the doubling to
within 0.1\% at five times the largest value in its training window and within
0.2\% at twenty times, while every boosted variant returned the training maximum
regardless of the input. That says the machinery works when the relationship is
of the assumed form; it does not say it works on dengue. The honest reading is that the
estimator removes the structural ceiling documented earlier, so a large error in
a record year can now be attributed to the data; it does not remove the risk of
a large error.

\emph{The classification threshold is a choice.} Exceeding the 75th percentile of
a unit's own history defines a large season; at the 90th percentile the positive
class becomes small enough that the balanced accuracy of any classifier falls and
the comparison between rungs loses power. The threshold is therefore a design
parameter, fixed before the runs and reported with them.

\emph{The exponent cannot exceed three.} The fitting routine bounds $\alpha$
above by $3$, so a tail steeper than that is reported as $3$ rather than as its
unconstrained maximum. This is immaterial here: no national or state-year size
fit approaches the bound, the largest values being $2.94$ at the state level
and $2.25$ nationally, and it is reached only by 12 of the 48{,}937 weekly fits
(0.02\%).
Every exponent reported in this paper is an unconstrained maximum.

\emph{Predictors are ecological and coarse.} No serotype composition, no
seroprevalence and no vector index enters the model, although all three are
plausible determinants of season size. To the extent that these are stable within
a unit, they are absorbed by unit-level effects; to the extent that they vary by
year, they are unmodelled heterogeneity that the scaling block may be
inadvertently proxying. This is a live hypothesis for the null reported here, and
it is the one we would test first.

\subsection{Implications for surveillance practice}

Three practices follow, none of them expensive.

First, report the tuple. An exponent accompanied by its unit, its $x_{\min}$, its
sample size and its comparison model can be assessed by a reader; an exponent
alone cannot, and the five levels here show that the same exponent can mean
opposite things.

Second, use the exceedance curve. Two parameters fitted to the current
cross-section give the probability that a municipality will exceed any level, and
the national fits reproduce the observed frequency of large seasons over two
decades. This is a cheap input to planning that requires no model training and no
history beyond the present year.

Third, do not translate a heavy tail into a claim of unpredictability without
specifying the loss. A distribution without a finite variance constrains
squared-error point forecasts and leaves probabilistic exceedance statements
intact --- and the exceedance statement is usually the one a health service needs.

\section{Conclusion}

The distribution of dengue epidemic size in Brazil is heavy-tailed with an
exponent close to two, and this is one of the few robust facts in the applied
literature on epidemic scaling: 16 of 16 national cross-sections, 96\% of 353 state-years, and a lower bound two to three orders of magnitude above the bulk.
Which of these statements a study reports, and whether it reports a power law or
an exponential at all, is determined by the construction it imposes on the data;
that heterogeneity is methodological before it is biological.

The consequence for forecasting is not the one usually drawn. Because
$\alpha\approx2$ fixes the entire exceedance curve, the fitted pair is a
two-parameter risk specification, and it is well calibrated against the empirical
frequency of large seasons over two decades of epidemic size. Posed as a
probability --- will this municipality exceed its own upper quartile? --- the
problem is tractable: lagged epidemic descriptors improve the Brier score over
calibrated persistence in 4 of 5 municipal origins, the only contrast here to
clear the pre-registered rule, and lift discrimination from $0.50$ to $0.65$ AUC,
while at state level the same contrast cannot be resolved on 25 units. Posed as a
magnitude, it is not: no rung achieves skill for size, and the scaling block
specifically adds nothing (0 of 5), its differential at the 95th percentile being
$+4.9\%$ in median with intervals that exclude zero in only 2 of 5 origins.

That last result is not an accident of the data. The exponent co-varies with the
lower bound and hence with the scale of the season, at $0.89$ nationally and
$0.64$ within states, in a way that simulation does not reproduce. The pair is
therefore a genuine low-variance summary of how large a season was, and for that
same reason a redundant input to any model that already knows how large the
previous one was. The redundancy was predicted from the coupling before the
ablation was run, and the ablation confirmed it.

What generalises beyond this dataset is the shape of the question. A heavy tail
does not make epidemics unpredictable; it bounds how well the magnitude of the
largest event can be estimated from second moments, and it leaves the probability
of exceeding any level fully specified. The exponent is worth reporting. It is
worth reporting as a risk curve, with its lower bound, and not as a predictor.
