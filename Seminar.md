# Seminar Title and Abstract

## Title

**Power Laws and Prediction Gaps: A Statistical Framework for Extreme Dengue Outbreaks in Brazilian Cities**

---

## Abstract

Dengue fever remains one of the most significant public health challenges in Brazil, with over 5,500 municipalities experiencing recurrent seasonal outbreaks of varying intensity. While traditional epidemiological models focus on average transmission dynamics, the distribution of outbreak magnitudes across cities exhibits a striking property: extreme events follow power law scaling. This heavy-tailed behavior implies that rare, catastrophic outbreaks are far more likely than predicted by normal distributions—yet these "black swan" events are precisely when public health systems are most vulnerable.

In this seminar, we present a comprehensive computational framework that bridges statistical physics with modern epidemiological surveillance. Using 15 years of weekly dengue case data from all Brazilian municipalities, we estimate power law scaling parameters (α) via maximum likelihood methods, identifying geographic regions where extreme outbreaks dominate the epidemic landscape. Our analysis reveals significant spatial heterogeneity in scaling behavior, with some states exhibiting thin-tailed distributions (high α) where outbreaks remain predictable, while others show heavy-tailed dynamics (low α) characterized by frequent extreme events.

Our analysis integrates four complementary approaches: (1) longitudinal tracking of α values to detect temporal shifts in epidemic regimes; (2) spatial clustering analysis using Local Moran's I to identify hotspots of extreme outbreak propensity; (3) correlation analysis linking scaling parameters to fundamental epidemiological drivers including R₀, epidemic duration, and timing; and (4) a machine learning predictive model that leverages historical power law patterns to forecast epidemic size, duration, and peak timing.

Key findings demonstrate that cities with lower α values (heavier tails) not only experience more extreme outbreaks but also show reduced predictability in standard epidemiological models—creating "prediction gaps" where traditional forecasting fails. By incorporating power law scaling factors as features in random forest models, we achieve improved out-of-sample forecasting performance for extreme events, with implications for proactive resource allocation and early warning systems.

This work demonstrates how methods from statistical physics can enhance epidemiological understanding of heavy-tailed outbreak dynamics across heterogeneous geographic landscapes.
